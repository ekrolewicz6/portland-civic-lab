# Portland's participatory budgeting measure, explained

**Measure 26-267 · November 3, 2026 · Independent analysis as of September 17, 2026**

**The choice:** Should Portland guarantee residents binding authority over a portion of public spending, with a permanent annual funding minimum, or retain the current budget process and consider alternatives without that mandate?

## What would change?

A YES vote would require the city to establish participatory budgeting, or PB. Residents would propose projects; staff and resident delegates would develop feasible choices; residents would vote; the city or its agents would implement the winners. An oversight committee would guide the process, and the City Auditor would periodically conduct an independent performance audit.

The annual minimum would equal **2% of the previous year's adopted General Fund discretionary ongoing expenses**. That is a specific budget category—not 2% of every dollar in Portland's total budget. Council could use other suitable city funding sources, subject to their restrictions. [Read the official amendment](https://www.portland.gov/auditor/elections/documents/pdx25ol-01-text-proposed-charter-change/download).

A NO vote leaves the existing process in place, including elected representatives, hearings, and testimony. It does not prohibit a future pilot, but it does not guarantee one either. [Official ballot filing](https://multco.us/file/measure_26-267/download).

## The strongest argument for YES

**People deserve some power to decide, not just opportunities to comment.** Residents can identify overlooked neighborhood needs, learn how budgets work, and see a direct connection between participation and public investment. Broad resident eligibility can include people excluded from ordinary elections. A protected allocation makes the program meaningful and harder to abandon.

This is more than a theoretical idea. Cambridge reports 10,172 participants in its 2026 cycle. A New York study found evidence of increased subsequent election participation among PB participants, although its observational design does not establish what Portland would experience. [Cambridge results](https://www.cambridgema.gov/news/2026/03/city-of-cambridge-announces-winning-projects-for-12th-participatory-budgeting-cycle); [New York study](https://researchers.kean.edu/en/publications/testing-the-participation-hypothesis-evidence-from-participatory-/).

**The tradeoff:** residents would secure this authority before knowing the detailed local operating plan or its results.

## The strongest argument for NO

**Test the program before locking in the funding obligation.** Portland faces pressure on existing services. A permanent floor reduces future budget flexibility, while important decisions about staffing, voting, project eligibility, and representation remain ahead. Participation can be uneven, and implementation can be expensive or slow.

City Club's 2025 research recommends a limited pilot and evaluation before broader adoption. That report predates this exact filing; it is useful evidence for the pilot-first argument, not a current official ruling on the measure. [City Club report](https://www.pdxcityclub.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/2025-03-14-Participatory-Budgeting-final.pdf).

**The tradeoff:** without a mandate, a pilot or stronger resident authority may never happen.

## Four facts that cut through the campaign language

1. **No new tax does not mean no cost.** PB uses money that could fund other priorities and requires administration. PB projects can themselves provide useful public services, so the entire allocation is not simply money lost to the public.
2. **The quoted $16.4 million is a preliminary first-year estimate.** The binding rule is a formula. Administration is included in program funding under the text; it should not automatically be added again as a separate required cost. [Ballot filing](https://multco.us/file/measure_26-267/download); [amendment](https://www.portland.gov/auditor/elections/documents/pdx25ol-01-text-proposed-charter-change/download).
3. **The amendment has safeguards, but not a complete rulebook.** Feasibility review, oversight, evaluation, and independent audits are required. Residency verification, voting disputes, detailed spending limits, and performance standards need further work. Operating rules can change; the charter funding floor has no sunset or express recession waiver. [Amendment](https://www.portland.gov/auditor/elections/documents/pdx25ol-01-text-proposed-charter-change/download).
4. **Portland's fiscal strain is real, but campaign budget snapshots need dates.** June's budget announcement projected about 100 layoffs; July's supplemental ordinance restored 30 positions. Those figures should not be turned into a purported final September layoff count. [June announcement](https://www.portland.gov/hello/news/2026/6/18/portland-city-council-adopts-final-budget-2026-27); [July ordinance](https://www.portland.gov/council/documents/ordinance/passed/192207).

## What remains uncertain?

The exact first-year calculation, funding sources, administrative budget, detailed participation rules, and eventual winners remain unresolved in this review. The full amendment starts funding by 2027–28 and the first PB process by July 2028, while the ballot summary says operational July 2027. Voters should not mistake setup for a completed project vote. [Full text](https://www.portland.gov/auditor/elections/documents/pdx25ol-01-text-proposed-charter-change/download); [ballot summary](https://multco.us/file/measure_26-267/download).

**A defensible YES prioritizes durable resident power and accepts the financial and implementation risks. A defensible NO prioritizes testing and flexibility, ideally with a concrete alternative.** The evidence supports PB's potential; it does not settle whether this particular commitment is best for Portland.
