# The Movable Dollar Plan

## Ten decisions that would change what Portland Public Schools can do for students

*Portland Civic Lab · September 2026 · Companion to "Where the Next Dollar Goes"*

---

## To the board member holding this

You govern an $862 million operating fund inside a $2.77 billion budget, with a deficit of more than $65 million already projected for next year [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p11], a workforce down 761 positions from 2021-22 (6,274 actual then, 5,513 budgeted now [tscc-review-fy2026 p3; budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p92]), an expired teacher contract in active successor bargaining, and a closure decision due within months. This plan proposes what to do about it, at motion level, in an order you can actually execute.

Two things make this document different from the plans that die in binders.

First, **it was built to be attacked, and it was.** Before this version, we convened a hostile review from seven perspectives: a district CFO, a union research director, a Black community advocate from North and Northeast Portland, an anti-closure parent organizer, a construction-industry bond veteran, a board general counsel, and a retired deputy superintendent who has buried plans like this one. They produced 58 objections, 18 of them fatal to the first draft. Where an objection was right, we changed the plan and say so. Where it was wrong, the answer is printed under the decision it attacks. What follows survived. The two most important concessions up front, because they reshape everything:

- **The teachers' contract expired June 30, 2026** [cba-pat-2023-2026 §31.1], which means successor bargaining is open *now*. Anything in this plan touching compensation, workload, calendar, or bargaining-unit staffing is a bargaining objective, not a board motion, and is labeled that way. A board that tries to adopt those items by resolution invites an unfair labor practice charge and hands away leverage for free.
- **Every reinvestment promise in the first draft collided with the $65 million deficit.** This version carries a reconciliation rule instead of a wish: in any cut year, verified savings go first against the deficit, and reinvestments are sized to audited savings, on a stated split, with a public tracking table. A plan that spends the same dollar twice is a press release.

Second, **it does not ask for trust.** Every factual claim traces to a document and page in the companion research [cited as docId p.N]; press-only figures are labeled press; judgments are ours and labeled. The district's own auditors just found six significant deficiencies and a Local Budget Law violation in the FY2025 close [fy25-acfr-memo], its citizen committee got nine working days to review $2.8 billion [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p2], and its last ten years of promises are catalogued, kept and broken, in the companion. This plan's enforcement is therefore never "the board promises." It is dedicated funds, published models before votes, named owners, and tripwires with stated consequences.

The shape: one governance precondition, a transparency package rebuilt for a depleted finance office, a capital-honesty standard aimed at the actor who actually breaks the chain, two reallocations sized honestly, two compounding bets with corrected math, an outside game aimed at the right legal venues, and a cross-cutting equity spine the first draft lacked.

# Decision 0 · Reconcile the plan with the board's own governance model

**The problem this solves:** Since 2025 this board has moved toward student outcomes focused governance, a framework that deliberately walls board attention off from operational detail. Half of this plan's directives sit at exactly the altitude that framework reserves for the superintendent. Passing them without touching the governance policies invites the two-word veto: "operational matter."

**The motion:** Amend board governance policy to expressly reserve as board-level matters: fiscal transparency standards and reporting cadence, budget development process and assumptions, capital program oversight, the contract portfolio above a stated threshold, and enrollment and attendance goal-setting. Where this plan directs operations, recast as goals-and-guardrails with mandatory reporting, which the governance model itself permits.

**Cost:** One policy amendment. **Authority:** The board owns its own governance policies.

**The objection we accepted:** A retired administrator called the first draft "operational directives from a board that adopted a governance model forbidding exactly that," with the policy amendment never sequenced. Correct. It is now Decision 0, first in time.

**The objection we push back on:** That outcomes-focused governance itself forbids this plan. It does not. A board cannot govern outcomes while blind to money; reserving fiscal visibility is not micromanagement, it is the precondition for every goal the framework asks the board to set. The governance literature the model rests on assumes functioning financial reporting. This district does not have it. First things first.

# Part One · See (the first 180 days)

## Decision 1 · Publish the books, at the speed the books can bear

**The motion:** (a) Appropriate funding for two to three finance and analytics positions, explicitly exempt from every savings target in this plan, with this decision's reporting calendar as their named workload; (b) beginning with the third quarter of FY2026-27 (or the second, if contracted support bridges the hiring), publish **quarterly** unaudited financial statements, fund-level revenues and expenditures against budget, each stamped "unaudited, subject to revision," with prior-year seasonal comparators, a plain-language seasonality note, and a published revision log so corrections are routine rather than scandals; (c) move to monthly publication only after one clean audit cycle; (d) publish the one-page budget decomposition, operating, capital, debt, side by side with ledger tags, in every budget document; (e) publish the eleven-year trend table (General Fund, all funds, enrollment, FTE) in every budget book; (f) report quarterly to the board in public session on the four or five assumptions that actually drove the last deficit: cost-of-living assumption, property tax collections, PERS, enrollment, state revenue; (g) publish, as a Decision 1 deliverable, the cost model for the 2023 strike settlement, whose roughly $175 million figure has circulated for three years as an unverified district statement [press; document.md §13 R3].

**Success metric, rebuilt:** Not "zero surprises," which a CFO can hit by concealing, sandbagging, or over-reserving. Instead: published forecast-accuracy bands, actuals within a stated tolerance of each key assumption, with misses explained in public within 30 days.

**The objections we accepted, and what they changed:** The CFO's case was better than our draft. The FY2025 audit found six significant federal deficiencies, staff turnover named as cause on finding after finding, and two violations of Oregon Local Budget Law, including expenditures over appropriations and a June appropriations change that legally required a supplemental budget [fy25-acfr-memo]. The district cut 96 central-office FTE and reduced central office staffing and services 14.82 percent [budget-fy2026-27-supt-message]. Ordering monthly statements in 30 days from that office guarantees errors, and in this climate every error becomes next month's "they lied" story; the transparency regime discredits itself in two quarters. So: capacity funded first, quarterly before monthly, revision logs by design. We also accepted that roughly 40 percent of General Fund revenue arrives in November-December, so raw monthly statements mislead without seasonal context; hence the comparators.

**The objection we push back on:** "Interim statements can mislead worse than silence." Only unframed ones. The district's own year just demonstrated what silence costs: a mid-year deficit its citizen committee confirmed [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p5], a reserve drained against a scheduled cliff [tscc-review-fy2026 p10], and assumptions flagged as unrealistic at adoption [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p4]. The audit findings are not a reason to delay transparency; they are the strongest evidence in the record that nobody, including the board, can currently see the money. And the claim that this is unaffordable fails against the plan's own math: two or three analysts cost well under one percent of what one badly-timed mid-year scramble costs in worst-option cuts.

## Decision 2 · Give the citizen reviewers a real seat, anchored where churn cannot reach

**The motion:** (a) Adopt, in board policy and in the published budget calendar itself, staged CBRC access: technical briefings and the assumptions package beginning in January, function and object detail as modules land in March and April, the formal proposed budget on the statutory clock, and a guaranteed minimum three-week review window with a two-week extension right; (b) adopt as policy that the board's written item-by-item response to every CBRC recommendation is published no later than the adoption meeting, with the adoption resolution reciting whether it was published and any failure explained in public session; (c) publish the local option levy's spending by function and object annually, which the committee needs to perform the compliance role the voters were promised [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p4]; (d) request that TSCC note Decision 1 and 2 artifact compliance in its statutory annual review, putting an outside witness on the promise; (e) during the consolidation cycle, provide standing CBRC testimony slots for representatives of affected school clusters.

**The objections we accepted:** The CFO is right that nine days is partly Salem's fault, not disrespect: the state revenue forecast and, in odd years, the actual appropriation land in May and June, and adoption is bracketed at June 30. A flat "four weeks earlier" mandate means reviewing staler numbers. Staged access solves what a shifted deadline cannot. And two reviewers observed that a standing rule from a board that has ignored four years of written recommendations binds nobody; hence policy rather than resolution, the calendar itself as the instrument, TSCC as witness, and adoption conditioned on the response being published.

**The objection we could not fully answer:** Nothing prevents a future board from amending the policy. True. The honest claim is narrower: this design makes abandonment a visible public act rather than a silent lapse. That is the strongest available enforcement short of statute, and we say so rather than pretending "binding" means binding.

## Decision 3 · Aim the capital-honesty standard at the actor who breaks the chain

**The finding that rebuilt this decision:** Our first draft required "independent cost validation before ballot numbers." The bond veteran's answer was devastating and documented: validation already existed, every time. In 2017, a professional cost estimator and OSM's own staff produced a market-aligned model near $678 million, and executive leadership cut roughly $100 million from it for the ballot, over staff and consultant objection, with no documented rationale [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-1-phase-1 p9-14]. And it happened again for the 2025 measure, in plain view: design-stage estimates of roughly $491M, $481M, and $455M for Jefferson, Cleveland, and Ida B. Wells sat in the oversight committee's packet while ballot-facing targets near $360-366M were set, for which, in the auditors' words, leadership "could not provide rationale or methodology" [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6 p20-28]. The problem was never missing information. It is that nothing binds the district to its own estimates.

**The motion:** (a) Adopt the binding rule: no ballot or program figure may be set below the current validated estimate range unless the board first adopts, in public session, a line-item reconciliation of every reduction, scope removed, escalation assumption changed, contingency cut, and the variance is disclosed in the explanatory statement voters see; (b) **rebaseline the 2025 program publicly within six months**, reconciled line by line from the schematic-design estimates, enumerating what scope the adopted targets actually buy, because tracking against unreconciled targets sets the program up to "fail honestly" against numbers nobody defended; (c) repair the oversight that exists before building more: fill the Bond Accountability Committee's long-vacant seats, publish its minutes, adopt formal protocols, and route its existing quarterly reports and the annual audit to the full board and the public site, all of which are the external auditors' own standing recommendations [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6, oversight findings]; (d) publish quarterly estimates-at-completion as **ranges**, decomposed against a named construction cost index so published-index escalation is separated from controllable residual, with a counsel-reviewed disclosure lag and an aggregate program-level claims reserve for projects in active dispute, because a point estimate on a claims-laden project is a litigation document; (e) put audit cadence in the engagement letter, final report due within a fixed number of months of fiscal year end, so a sixteen-month publication gap like calendar 2022 cannot recur; (f) **deliver, not just report**: ratify the $60 million Center for Black Student Excellence allocation as a floor, with a scope decision no later than June 2027 and a published spend-down schedule; commit that any Jefferson de-scoping option is developed with the community at the table and published before adoption; and make CBSE and Jefferson the first two projects tracked under the new quarterly reporting, as commitments being carried to completion rather than cautionary tales.

**The objection that earned clause (f):** the equity reviewer observed that the CBSE, $60 million allocated in 2020, zero spent through February 2025, auditors now openly inviting the district to "revisit whether the same capital need exists" [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6 p33-34], is how promises to Black Portland die: delay it yourself, then discover the need has changed. And that a cost-honesty regime arriving exactly when Jefferson, the community's only high school, sits paused at $491 million against a $311 million promise, functions as a ratchet that de-scopes the Black school first. A standard that finds religion on the Black school's turn is not a standard. Clause (f) exists so the discipline and the delivery are the same motion.

**The objection we push back on:** "Quarterly EACs create sandbagging." Partially answered by ranges, index decomposition, and grading teams on residual rather than raw variance. The remainder is a real cost of transparency we accept knowingly: some padding risk, in exchange for ending a regime in which the district's own board did not receive the quarterly reports that already existed [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6, oversight findings]. The counter-record also matters: Lincoln finished $16.9M under revised budget, Kellogg and McDaniel near target [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6 p13]. This machine hits numbers it states honestly. The standard's job is to make honest statement the only kind there is.

# Part Two · Reallocate (sized honestly this time)

## Decision 4 · Move real dollars toward students, on a metric that cannot be gamed

**The confession first:** Our first draft promised a $26 million redirect and scored this decision "cost: negative." The red team dismantled that arithmetic from three directions, and all three were right. The MAP contract nets well under its $218,000 headline because the state's free assessments cover only grades 3-8 [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p10]. Part of the instructional-coach growth is restricted grant money, cutting it frees no General Fund dollar, and the citizen committee itself carved out early-literacy coaches as worth keeping [cbrc-2024-25-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p6]. The purchased-services spikes include one-time consolidation moving costs, litigation, and audit scope that cannot be "renegotiated" into classrooms. Worst of all, our own success metric was self-defeating: counselors, librarians, and attendance staff are *support-services* functions under Oregon's chart of accounts, so buying the very things this plan wants pushes the "instructional share" metric down. The engine contradicted the dashboard.

**The motion, rebuilt:** (a) Commission the benchmarking study the citizen committee asked for, against districts with similar language-learner rates, labor costs, and building age [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p5], and **let the study set the target**; (b) define the plan's metric as **dollars reaching schools and students**: instruction *plus* direct student supports (counseling, library, health, attendance), computed on a frozen chart-of-accounts crosswalk certified annually by the external auditor, so the number can be moved only by real redirection, never by recoding; (c) adopt a first-installment savings list at its honest size, likely $2-5 million of true General Fund money, each line verified as recurring and rebiddable before it is claimed, and route those first dollars by a distribution rule: highest-need schools first, reported school by school, responsive to the state audit's finding that staff exit the highest-Black-and-Latino-enrollment schools at twice the rate of the lowest [sos-audit-2019-01]; (d) apply symmetry of specificity: a hard dollar rollback with published justification on central administration and management-services growth, adopted with the same teeth as anything touching a classroom; (e) direct the superintendent to return within 60 days with executable plans, MAP termination terms *conditioned on a certified replacement pathway for TAG identification and any other program the assessment gates* (the district is under a state corrective action plan for TAG and the free state test is not a norm-referenced identification instrument), a coach-deployment review reaching any new ratio through attrition and contractual transfer processes with impact bargaining acknowledged, and a rebid plan for flagged contract lines, and the board votes on the returned plans.

**Removed from this decision entirely:** overload pay. The contract both mandates it and says, jointly, that "overload numbers are not a staffing model" [cba-pat-2023-2026 §8.3.2, §8.3.6]; the union designed it as a penalty so understaffing would cost money. The only lawful way it falls is staffing classrooms properly, which costs more than the premium. It is now what it should always have been: this plan's best leading indicator of understaffing, a metric, not a source of funds. The analysis comparing overload cost to staffing cost, which the citizen committee requested and never received [cbrc-2025-26-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p5], is a Decision 1 deliverable.

**The objection we push back on:** That any redirection during a deficit is fantasy. The rollback in (d) and the honest list in (c) are small, which is the point: this decision no longer claims to fund the plan. It claims to move the district's spending mix toward students at whatever speed verified dollars allow, on a metric that cannot lie, with the study, not a slogan, setting the destination. Slower and true beats fast and fake, in this district especially, where the fast-and-fake version would be recoded into compliance within a year.

## Decision 5 · The footprint correction: one decision, phased execution, promises sized to audited savings

**What the red team broke, and how this version holds:** Five reviewers attacked the first draft's closure decision and every major blow landed. The counselor-and-librarian pledge was unpriced; priced, it runs $6.5-9 million recurring, roughly all of a modest wave's net savings, meaning the closures would do nothing for the deficit that justifies them. "Bind by resolution" binds nobody; this district's record contains a 10 percent reserve policy pinned at 5, a pension reserve spent in one year, and a 2019 promise to the state of a right-sizing process "effective by 2020-21" that is still "planning" in 2026 [tscc-review-fy2026 p10; sos-audit-2019-01 board response; budget-fy2026-27-supt-message]. The one-wave-by-2027 timeline made the community guarantee chronologically impossible, the final year had already started. The savings model was to be trusted un-validated in a plan that requires validation for every bond estimate. And a wave selected by utilization alone re-selects North and Northeast Portland, because under-enrollment there is the residue of the district's own history; thirteen of the twenty lowest-density buildings in the state audit's map were Title I schools [sos-audit-2019-01 p25].

**The motion, rebuilt:** (a) Adopt closure criteria in public **before any list exists**, with historic under-investment as an explicit counterweight criterion and a stated constraint on concentration in any one cluster; (b) publish the per-building savings model, **independently validated to the same standard Decision 3 applies to bond estimates**, against a required-contents checklist: transition costs including contractual moving compensation [cba-pat-2023-2026 §19.2-19.5], transportation, mothballing, bond-investment history per building (closing a building the 2017 health-and-safety money just fixed needs an answer before the meeting, not after), a flight-sensitivity table at 0, 5, and 10 percent displaced-family attrition, and ten-year net; (c) publish alongside it a racial-equity impact analysis under the board's own 2011 Racial Educational Equity Policy, as that policy contemplates; (d) decide the list as **one decision with phased effective dates over two to three years**, no school closing before 2028-29, so that every affected community receives its transition plan, transportation impact statement, and building-reuse path *before* the final year begins, per school, as a condition of appearing on the list; this supersedes any earlier effective date in the district's current consolidation timeline, and the board should say so plainly when adopting criteria; the published, validated model receives a minimum 45-day public review with a hearing for each listed school before the vote; and the vote is accompanied by a one-time transition budget line with a named funding source, not an assumption that transition pays for itself; (e) size the reinvestment honestly: verified net savings flow first against the deficit, with a stated share, we recommend 30 percent, escrowed in a dedicated fund whose appropriations are restricted to named student-facing positions, **receiving schools of affected communities funded first**, tracked in a public savings-to-positions table with an annual CBRC compliance review published against it; (f) adopt the tripwire: any subsequent phase of closures is suspended if the prior phase's escrowed positions go unfunded; (g) condition the list vote itself on Decision 1 performing, at least one quarter of on-time interim statements and one public assumptions report first, and on the CBRC receiving the validated model on Decision 2's access standard.

**What we no longer claim:** that closures materially solve the deficit (the honest model will likely show $1-2 million net per elementary, before flight risk), or that any resolution "binds" a future board. The case for consolidation stands on different ground: geometry. Revenue arrives per student; costs arrive per building; the district's own message commits to consolidation [budget-fy2026-27-supt-message]; and every year of drift pays full fixed costs on empty seats out of the same fund that eliminated 322 positions in the adopted budget, of 336 proposed [budget-fy2026-27-supt-message; press]. The honest sales pitch to communities is not "this fixes the budget." It is: "here is the validated model, here is the equity analysis, here is what your children's receiving school gets first, here is the tripwire if we break our word, and here is why doing nothing quietly taxes every remaining classroom."

**The objection we could not fully answer:** the parent organizer's last line, that a future board can un-vote anything. True, and said plainly in the document communities will read. The escrow, the tracking table, the tripwire, and the public model make breach visible and costly. Nothing available to a school board makes breach impossible. Communities deserve that sentence in writing, so it is in this one.

# Part Three · Compound (the two bets, with corrected math)

## Decision 6 · Run enrollment as the long game it actually is

**The correction that matters:** Our first draft claimed a recovered kindergartner pays "almost immediately" under the state's higher-of-two-years funding rule. Backwards. In a *declining* district the state already pays on the prior-year count, PPS is funded on 51,390 weighted enrollment against roughly 41,700 actual [budget-fy2026-27-vol1] , so a marginal recovered student adds nothing until recovery outruns the annual decline of roughly 800. The honest model: near-zero marginal revenue in years one and two, then compounding for a decade as recovered cohorts prop up every future year's count. This is still one of the strongest long plays available to the district. It is not a budget patch, and a plan that sold it as one would die at the CFO's first slide.

**The motion, rebuilt:** (a) Stand up a small enrollment function missioned on **kindergarten capture rate, disaggregated by race and by cluster, with targets set on closing the gaps between them**, and with the rate's methodology and denominator published as a versioned series produced *outside* the enrollment office (district research or PSU directly), because an office graded on one number it also computes will find two points somewhere; (b) direct the superintendent to negotiate an intergovernmental agreement with Multnomah County covering kindergarten-transition supports and consent-based data sharing with Preschool for All, the IGA itself is the metric, and the word "feeder" appears nowhere, because the county's program is provider-neutral by design and treating it as a PPS pipeline turns a partner into an adversary; (c) **offer** a structured exit interview to every departing family, publish an annual aggregated and de-identified analysis with response rates and minimum-cell suppression, and route findings about discipline and belonging into an accountability loop, not just a marketing one; (d) protect the visible K-5 commitments, librarians and arts universally, and TAG **only paired with universal screening, demographic reporting, and corrective-action-plan compliance in the same clause**, because protecting the district's historically whitest program without reform is not retention strategy, it is subsidy.

**The objection that reshaped (d), on the record:** the equity reviewer: "'Storefront' tells me exactly who the customer is imagined to be, and it is not us." The framing is gone. The substance survives in a form that serves every family: the things being protected are the things *all* families see and use, and the capture-rate mission is now explicitly a gap-closing mission, so the office cannot hit its number by courting only the cheapest-to-recover families.

## Decision 7 · Attendance: the cheapest outcome win, done in a way communities will accept

**The motion, rebuilt:** (a) Adopt a board goal on chronic absenteeism, currently above 36 percent, ten points over the national average [cbrc-2025-26-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p1], with quarterly reporting **by school and disaggregated by race**, an annual attendance-coding audit on a sample of schools so the number cannot be gamed, and a firewall: attendance data is never a closure criterion; (b) fund attendance supports at the highest-absence schools specified as **credentialed social workers, family-engagement staff, and contracted community-based organizations where district trust is broken, with an explicit no-law-enforcement-referral commitment**, because the highest-absence schools are the communities with generations of experience of what districts historically sent about attendance; (c) pursue calendar coherence, the district currently runs only 16 five-day weeks of 38 [cbrc-2025-26-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p7-8], **as a named objective in successor bargaining, honestly priced**, because the fragmented weeks are substantially bargained professional development, planning, grading, and conference structure [cba-pat-2023-2026 §6.3-6.8], and the contract requires Association consent for aggregate workday changes; the board cannot rebuild the calendar by motion, and this plan will not pretend it can; (d) treat transportation reliability as an attendance intervention and report it as one.

**What changed and why:** Both the union's and counsel's reviews established that the first draft's calendar clause was an unfair-labor-practice charge in waiting, a unilateral change to a mandatory subject during an expired-contract status quo [cba-pat-2023-2026 §31.1, §6.8.1; PECBA]. The union's substantive point was also fair: those "fragmented" days are preparation time that *is* instruction. So the ask moves to the table, priced, with the union as co-author, and the union's own statement that "our members hate fragmented weeks too" suggests a deal exists. Meanwhile (a), (b), and (d) need nobody's consent and start now.

# Part Four · The outside game

## Decision 8 · Go to Salem with the right asks in the right venues

**The corrections:** Three reviewers converged on the same legal error in our first draft: Measure 5 compression lives in the Oregon Constitution, Article XI section 11b, and its interaction with local option levies in Measure 50. No bill fixes it. The success metric "a bill number" was legally impossible for that ask. Separately, the labor reviewer was right that unilaterally dropping the Quality Education Model adequacy frame, the only frame with constitutional footing under Article VIII section 8, isolates PPS from every coalition partner it needs, and the equity reviewer was right that a three-ask agenda with no defense of the equity streams reads as abandoning them.

**The motion, rebuilt:** Adopt a legislative agenda with the adequacy case as its umbrella and three quantified asks inside it: (a) **special education**: raise the 11 percent funding cap *jointly with a raised appropriation*, in coalition with the state's largest districts, because a cap raise inside a fixed pie just reslices scarcity; PPS serves 17 percent [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p11]; (b) **compression**: pursue a constitutional referral, House or Senate Joint Resolution, exempting voter-approved local option levies from Measure 5 compression, built as a multi-biennium statewide campaign with the coalition of compression-losing districts TSCC's own tables identify, *paired with an achievable statutory fallback*, a state backfill mechanism for compression losses, so the two-session horizon contains something winnable; the leak is $53.4 million projected for FY2026-27, and the teachers levy delivers $1.51 of its voted $1.99 [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p28; tscc-annual-report-2024-25-general p50]; (c) **PERS**: state assumption of school-district rate increases above a stated threshold, with the statewide fiscal note printed beside the ask; the district's blended rate is moving from about 4 percent toward 23 as side accounts expire [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p10]. Add two planks: marginal dollars won in Salem flow through the district's equity allocation first, and the district defends the Student Success Act equity accounts and the Black Student Success streams by name. Add the levy: the 2029 local option renewal is a named board workstream beginning 2028, running on the trust ledger Decisions 1-5 either build or burn.

**Success metrics, corrected:** a coalition letter and a filed bill for (a) and (c) within two sessions; a filed joint resolution plus a named statewide coalition for (b); and the fallback backfill bill as the near-term win.

# Decision 9 · The equity spine (cross-cutting, and an admission)

The first draft of this plan contained eight decisions and did not once name the board's own 2011 Racial Educational Equity Policy. For a district whose state audit found it performing worse than most national peers for Black students while doing fine for white students [sos-audit-2019-01 p29], in a city whose school closures have historically landed on Black and brown neighborhoods, that omission was not neutral. That is what an efficiency lens does when nobody checks it. We are keeping the reviewer's sentence that fixed it: *per-student arithmetic that never asks which students is how this district has always hurt mine.*

**The motion:** Every metric this plan creates, capture rate, absenteeism, dollars-reaching-students, closure savings and reinvestment, bond delivery, is reported disaggregated by race and school poverty band, on the same schedule as the aggregate. Every implementing resolution includes the equity analysis the 2011 policy contemplates. The closure criteria carry the historic-under-investment counterweight (Decision 5a). The reinvestment escrow funds receiving schools of affected communities first (Decision 5e). The CBSE floor and Jefferson commitment are ratified (Decision 3f). The equity allocation, halved in 2024-25 without the return-on-investment analysis the citizen committee twice requested, gets that analysis published before any further change in either direction.

**Cost:** Reporting configuration. This is the cheapest decision in the plan and the one whose absence would have discredited all the others.

# The labor-relations page (read before voting anything)

The PAT agreement expired June 30, 2026; successor bargaining is open; status quo obligations apply [cba-pat-2023-2026 §31.1, §8.3.1]. Under Oregon's collective bargaining law, unilateral change to mandatory subjects during status quo is an unfair labor practice. Accordingly:

| Plan element | Character | Vehicle |
|---|---|---|
| Financial reporting, budget calendar, CBRC terms (D1, D2) | Not bargaining subjects | Board policy, now |
| Bond standards, BAC repair, CBSE/Jefferson (D3) | Not bargaining subjects | Board policy/resolution, now |
| MAP termination, contract rollbacks (D4) | Management authority, with impact effects | Superintendent returns executable plans; impact bargaining where triggered |
| Coach deployment changes (D4e) | Bargaining-unit staffing | Attrition and contractual transfer processes; impact bargaining; savings on CBA timelines, not budget-cycle wishes |
| Overload pay | Contractual compensation | A metric in this plan, never a motion |
| Closure transition effects (D5) | Effects bargaining territory | Jointly planned transition process, published per school |
| Calendar (D7c) | Mandatory subject | Successor-bargaining objective, priced, co-designed |
| Attendance goal, reporting, supports (D7a,b,d) | Not bargaining subjects | Board goal, now |

The first draft blurred these lines. A board member who votes this plan should know exactly which votes are free and which are opening positions in a negotiation, because the union certainly will.

# What this plan does not claim

It does not claim closures fix the deficit; the deficit outlasts them. It does not claim the redirect is $26 million; it is whatever the verified list proves, starting small. It does not claim enrollment recovery pays this year; it pays this decade. It does not claim a resolution binds the next board; it claims breach will be public, priced, and tripwired. It does not claim the district can fix compression, the SPED cap, or PERS alone; it names the venues and the coalitions. And it does not claim neutrality between students and any adult interest: where this plan errs, it is built to err toward the classroom, visibly, in ways a stranger can audit.
