=== 1. [FATAL] ? on D1: You are ordering monthly published financials from an office that just failed its annual close
The FY2024-25 audit produced six federal significant deficiencies — a misstated SEFA, misclassified grant revenue in two funds, missing indirect-cost and time-and-effort documentation — with 'staff turnover' named as the cause on finding after finding, plus two violations of Oregon Local Budget Law: expenditures exceeding appropriations by fund and function, and a budget-transfer process used where a supplemental budget was legally required [fy25-acfr-memo]. The district cut 96 central office FTE and reduced central office 14.82% in the same period [budget-fy2026-27-supt-message]. The plan's claim that the district 'closes its books monthly already ... or could not produce an ACFR' is a non sequitur — an ACFR requires one annual close, and the auditors just showed this shop misstating numbers at annual-close standard. Monthly unaudited statements in 30 days from this office will contain errors; in Portland's current climate every error becomes next month's 'they lied' story, staff go defensive, and the transparency regime discredits itself within two quarters. The plan itself lists 'finance and analytics capacity' on its start list as the most expensive absence — and then assigns that same depleted office the work of Decisions 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7, roughly ten new recurring public reporting products, with zero dollars attached.
FIX: Sequence it: fund named finance positions in the same resolution (the plan claims 'cost: staff time' — put a number on it), start with quarterly statements, move to monthly only after one clean audit cycle, and stamp every interim release 'unaudited, subject to revision' with a published revision log so corrections are routine rather than scandals.

=== 2. [FATAL] ? on D3: The $60M Center for Black Student Excellence is your Exhibit A but never your motion — and your 'honesty standard' arrives exactly on Jefferson's turn
Voters approved $60 million for the Center for Black Student Excellence in 2020; through February 2025 the district had spent zero, hired a director only in fall 2024, and the auditors — after flagging it three years running — are now openly inviting the district to 'revisit whether the same capital need exists or if the district has different demands' [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6 p33-34]. That sentence is how promises to Black Portland die: delay it yourself, then discover the need has changed. This plan quotes the CBSE stall as evidence for process reform and contains not one word committing to deliver it — no scope deadline, no spend-down schedule, no protection of the $60 million as a floor against 'cost validation' shrinkage; under D3's own regime, the easiest project to keep on budget is one you never start. And look at the sequencing of this new discipline: Lincoln got built first, full scope, finishing $16.9M under its revised budget [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6 p13] — and the era of independent validation and quarterly estimates-at-completion begins precisely when Jefferson, the only high school my community has, sits paused at a $491M estimate against a $311M promise [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6 p21-22]. Cost honesty that the district finds religion on only when it is the Black school's turn is not a standard; it is a ratchet that will de-scope Jefferson and quietly reabsorb the CBSE.
FIX: Add delivery motions, not just reporting motions: a board resolution ratifying the $60M CBSE allocation as a floor with a scope decision by a date certain and a published spend-down schedule; a Jefferson restart commitment in which any de-scoping option is developed with the community at the table and published before adoption; and make CBSE and Jefferson the first two projects under the new quarterly reporting, framed as commitments being tracked to completion, not cautionary tales.

=== 3. [FATAL] ? on D3: Independent validation already existed at every ballot — and leadership cut below it anyway, including three months ago
I testified at TSCC when the Year 1 audit came out, so let me say what it actually found: the 2017 numbers WERE independently validated. A professional cost estimator produced $403.5M in hard costs, OSM operational staff built a $678M total model aligned with market conditions, and then OSM executive leadership cut $100M off the top with no documented rationale — over the objections of its own staff and program management consultant [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-1-phase-1 p9-11]. Your motion (a) mandates a step the district has performed on every bond since 2017 and does not bind the only actor who has ever broken the chain. Worse, the district repeated the identical move for the very 2025 bond your success metric targets, in plain view: third-party estimators and CMGCs on every modernization team produced $490M+ for Jefferson, $481M for Cleveland, $455M for Ida B. Wells at 100 percent schematic design, and the superintendent set ballot-facing targets of $366M and $360M for which, in the auditors' words, leadership 'could not provide rationale or methodology' [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6 p20-25]. The validated estimates sat in the December 2024 BAC packet next to the targets when the board voted the measure onto the ballot. Requiring more validation is asking for a second thermometer while the patient sets the reading. This is not an information problem and it never was; it is a political-will problem, and Decision 3(a) as written would have changed nothing in 2017, 2020, or 2025.
FIX: Replace the validation requirement with a rule that binds the board itself: no ballot or program number may be set below the current independent estimate unless the board adopts, in public session, a line-item reconciliation of every reduction (scope removed, escalation assumption changed, contingency cut), and the variance is disclosed in the explanatory statement voters see. Pair it with a mandatory public rebaseline of the 2025 program now, since its ballot targets are already ~$100M per school below the district's own schematic-design estimates.

=== 4. [FATAL] ? on D3: Quarterly public EACs on claims-laden projects are a discovery gift and a sandbagging engine — and the quarterly reports already exist
Benson is in active claims right now: the contractor filed 'significant claims' against PPS (disclosed at the January 2025 BAC meeting, dollar amount withheld), and OSM has filed its own warranty notice and notice of claim over the gym flood, with causation 'still under investigation' [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6 p13-14]. A published quarterly EAC on a project in that posture is a litigation document. If the public number includes claim exposure, you have handed the contractor's lawyers PPS's own valuation of the dispute and put a floor under every settlement conversation; if it excludes it, your 'honesty standard' compels the district to publish a number everyone in the OAC meetings knows is wrong — institutionalized dishonesty inside the honesty decision. Any competent general counsel will carve the active-dispute projects out of the table in the first executive session, which means the standard dies precisely where it matters. Second-order effect every owner's rep knows: teams graded quarterly on EAC-versus-budget variance pad baselines and park known growth in unbooked pending change orders, so the public table lags the real number in e-Builder — the sandbag becomes the system. And the mechanism largely exists: OSM already delivers quarterly status reports with EACs and contingency balances to the BAC [year-6 p14, fn10, fn12]; the auditors' actual finding was that those reports never reached the full board [year-6, oversight section p41]. You are proposing a new pipe while the existing one lies disconnected on the floor.
FIX: Publish EAC ranges with an aggregate program-level claims reserve rather than project-level point estimates; adopt a counsel-reviewed disclosure lag for projects in active dispute; and make the routing fix the centerpiece — the existing BAC quarterly report goes to the full board and the public site, per the auditors' own recommendation.

=== 5. [FATAL] ? on D4: The overload-pay 'savings' are contractually impossible and arithmetically backwards
The plan lists 'overload pay as a staffing strategy' on its stop list and treats the growth from $1.7M to $4M+ as a redirection candidate, but overload pay is not a discretionary budget line — it is Article 8 compensation the district is bound to pay: 'The District will continue to pay overages in the 2023-24 school year and all subsequent years' [cba-pat-2023-2026 §8.3.6]. The contract already says, jointly, what the plan pretends to discover: 'Both parties recognize and agree that overload numbers are not a staffing model' [§8.3.2] — we wrote that penalty precisely so understaffing would cost the district money. The only lawful way overload pay falls is hiring more classroom teachers, and staffing a classroom costs more than the overage premium on it, which is why the CBRC asked the district to 'provide an analysis comparing the cost of overload pay to the cost of additional' staffing [cbrc-2025-26-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p5] — an analysis that does not exist. So Decision 4's headline — 'Cost: Negative. This decision funds others' — is false on its own success metric ('overload pay falling as classrooms are staffed properly'), which requires net new spending, not a redirect. A plan that counts a contractual penalty for understaffing as recoverable waste is telling the board it can cut its way out of a bill that only proper staffing retires.
FIX: Remove overload pay from the stop list and from the redirection arithmetic entirely. Recast it as the plan's best leading indicator of understaffing — a metric, not a source of funds — and state that reducing it requires the staffing investment the CBRC asked to see costed. Decision 4's 'Cost: Negative' claim must be requalified.

=== 6. [FATAL] ? on D4: The $26M redirection is built on line items that cannot legally or arithmetically produce it
Walk the named installments: MAP is $218,000 a year — 0.8% of the target [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p10]. The instructional-coach growth includes coaches funded by the state Early Literacy Grant — cutting grant-funded FTE frees no General Fund dollar — and the CBRC itself carved out early-grade coaches as worth keeping [cbrc-2024-25-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p6, recommendation 5]. And the flagship item, the 199% Management Services jump, sits inside a 61.9% all-funds Purchased Services surge [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p9] that the plan's own companion document says is 'two-thirds bond' [document.md W3] — and that same companion states the iron rule: bond dollars becoming teachers is 'legally impossible, every time, no exceptions.' The 393% Other Property Services and 1,452% Professional Moving Services jumps in the same table are the consolidation program moving itself; you cannot rebid your way from those codes into classrooms. So the realizable General Fund content of D4(c) is perhaps $3-5M against a $26M promise, and D7 and D6 are supposedly funded from it. Worse, an instructional-share board target is the single most recodable metric in school finance: the fastest path to +3 points is reclassifying coaches and interventionists into instruction function codes, which staff will do, and which the same watchdogs will then call fraud.
FIX: Rebuild the target bottom-up from a General-Fund-only, object-level list with a dollar figure per item, separate all-funds and bond object codes explicitly, and compute the share metric on a frozen chart-of-accounts crosswalk certified annually by the external auditor so it can only be moved by real redirection, not recoding.

=== 7. [FATAL] ? on D4: The funding engine is a phantom pot, and the plan's own investments push its own metric backwards
Decision 4 is scored 'Cost: Negative. The '199% management services' line sits inside a proposed-year purchased-services jump whose components the CBRC itself lists — Professional Moving Services up 1,452%, Legal up 750%, Audit up 493% [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p9] — which any CFO reads instantly as one-time consolidation moves, litigation, and audit scope, plus object-code reclassification, not a rebiddable base of contracts; you cannot 'renegotiate' a moving bill for schools you are closing. Worse, the plan double-spends the pot: the $26M is 'redirected into classrooms' to close the instructional share gap, AND funds D7's attendance teams, AND presumably D6's enrollment office. And the killer: counselors, librarians, and attendance/social-work staff are Functions 2120, 2220, and 2110 — support services — under Oregon's chart of accounts, and NCES 'instruction' excludes student support services, so Decisions 5 and 7's marquee purchases mechanically push the plan's own success metric (instructional share) DOWN. The plan's engine room contradicts its dashboard.
FIX: Re-scope D4 honestly: separate (i) true General Fund savings (a realistic list, probably $2-5M) from (ii) function-shift reallocations, and price each downstream decision against (i) only. Replace the instructional-share target with a 'dollars reaching schools' metric that counts counselors, librarians, and attendance staff on the right side of the line, or explicitly define the target on an instruction-plus-student-supports basis. === 8. [FATAL] ? on D5: One wave is how you hollowed Albina the last time — and this plan has no equity screen on the list
I sat through the closures of the 2000s, and every one of them was justified by exactly this arithmetic: revenue per student, costs per building, small schools rank worst, and the small schools were ours — made small by district transfer policy and city-abetted displacement, then closed for being small. Your own companion research says it plainly: closures in this district's history 'have landed hardest on Black and brown North and Northeast Portland, which remembers' [document.md §09] — and then the recommendations document forgot it. Decision 5 orders a per-building savings model and a single-wave list with no racial equity impact analysis required before the vote, no closure criteria adopted in public before the list exists, no criterion for historic disinvestment, and no constraint on geographic concentration; a savings model without an equity screen will mechanically re-select my neighborhood, because under-enrollment is the residue of what was done to us. The 'one wave' logic is worse, not better, from where I sit: twenty closures at once, decided by early 2027, means no community has the capacity to contest its own school's case — a fait accompli dressed as efficiency, with the board's 2011 Racial Educational Equity Policy never once invoked. And the 'visible reinvestment' flows to the survivors: the schools that stay open get the counselor and the librarian, while the communities that bear the cost get a transition plan and a bus ride. Who bears the cost is the whole question, and this decision never asks it.
FIX: Require, in the motion itself: a published racial-equity impact analysis under the 2011 Racial Educational Equity Policy alongside the savings model before any vote; closure criteria adopted publicly before any list, with historic under-investment as an explicit counterweight criterion; a stated constraint on concentration in any one cluster; and first-priority reinvestment (the counselor, the librarian, and program guarantees) directed to the receiving schools of affected communities, named in the same resolution as the closures. Without the equity analysis requirement, nothing saves this decision for me.

=== 9. [FATAL] ? on D5: The flagship promise is unpriced, and every number we do have says closures can't pay for it
You want my community to accept a closure in exchange for 'a counselor and a librarian in every remaining school,' and you have priced that promise at exactly zero dollars. I priced it. Counselors are allocated at 350:1, so most elementaries carry a fraction of one [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p231-232]; middle schools are budgeted 0.50 teacher-librarian by the district's own staffing table [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p231]; elementary library staffing is down 31 percent with libraries dark several days a week [cbrc-2024-25-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p6-7]. Topping roughly 70 remaining schools up to a full-time counselor and full-time librarian is on the order of 45-60 licensed FTE, and with a 4.18 percent blended PERS rate plus an 18.79 percent UAL fringe on every salary [budget-fy2026-27-vol1], that is $6.5-9M per year, recurring, forever. Your own companion document says per-building savings are 'on the order of a principal, office and custodial staffing, and utilities per building, minus transition costs' and are 'real but small' against a projected deficit above $65M [document.md W2, D6; budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p11] — meaning the promise consumes roughly all of the gross savings before you subtract transition compensation the PAT contract mandates [cba-pat-2023-2026 §19.2-19.5], added busing, and mothballing on buildings nobody has a reuse plan for. So either the resolution is broken in the first hard budget cycle, or the closures do nothing for the deficit that is the stated reason for closing. You cannot have both, and the plan never chooses.
FIX: Invert the sequence and the sizing: publish the independently validated per-building net savings model first, then write the reinvestment resolution with a specific FTE count, a specific dollar figure, and the named fund line it draws from — sized to audited net savings, not to a slogan. If the model shows the savings can't buy the promise, say so before any community is asked to vote.

=== 11. [FATAL] ? on D5: 'One wave' means twenty schools, minutes of scrutiny each, and a district-wide staffing churn in a single fall
This plan spends Decision 2 proving that nine working days to review a budget makes oversight 'symbolic rather than substantive' [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p2] — and then Decision 5 proposes deciding up to twenty school closures [document.md, press-reported scale] in one wave, on a savings model the plan itself only commissions in the same 90-day window as the vote the board has committed to by early 2027. Twenty schools in one vote is not political efficiency; it is a scrutiny budget of minutes per school, in which no community can get a per-building error caught before it becomes permanent. And the wave does not stay inside the closed schools: the PAT contract says of closures that 'usually there are not enough positions and the remaining professional educators are unassigned' [cba-pat-2023-2026 §18.6.9], so a twenty-school wave dumps hundreds of educators into simultaneous seniority-driven placement, bumping staff at receiving schools and schools that were never on any list. The plan's 'each fight spends the same political capital' claim is asserted, not evidenced — it is exactly the kind of unlabeled judgment the plan promises to label — and it prices a twenty-school wave's compounding chaos at zero.
FIX: Cap wave one, or keep one decision date but stagger effective dates over two to three years with per-school public hearings and a minimum 90-day public review period on the published model. If 'one wave' must survive, it survives only with the same review standard the plan demands for a budget one-twentieth as consequential per family.

=== 12. [FATAL] ? on D5: The closure savings are already spent — on the $65M deficit
The plan opens by acknowledging a projected deficit above $65M, then binds closure savings by resolution to roughly 100 new licensed positions — a counselor and librarian in every remaining school is on the order of $10-15M recurring at a loaded cost near $140k per FTE with PERS heading to 23% [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p10]. Net recurring General Fund savings per closed elementary run $1-2M at best once you remember teachers move with their students and only admin, custodial, and utilities are truly avoidable — so the pledge consumes the entire savings of an 8-10 building wave, and the closures contribute exactly nothing to the structural deficit that justified them, which means the $65M still comes out of classrooms anyway. The plan also waves off transition costs: the current proposed budget already carries a $2.4M, 1,452% increase in moving services for a limited consolidation [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p9], the PAT contract entitles every moved educator to paid packing hours and extended-hours compensation [cba-pat-2023-2026 §19.2-19.5], and receiving schools with regional special-education programs need accessibility and program-space work that bond funds legally cannot cover if the projects are not on the bond list. And 'one wave' is not just political courage — it is boundary redraws, transportation re-routing under a driver shortage, and IEP-driven program relocations executed simultaneously, which this central office, down 142 FTE this year alone [budget-fy2026-27-supt-message], cannot deliver with published transition plans 'before the school's final year begins.'
FIX: Cap the reinvestment pledge at a stated share (say 25-30%) of independently verified net recurring savings, first-call but not total-call, with the remainder scored against the deficit; sequence the wave as one DECISION but a two-year phased execution; and add a one-time transition budget line with a named funding source before the vote.

=== 13. [FATAL] ? on D6: 'Storefront' protection makes TAG — the whitest program in this district — a protected class, while the plan never mentions the equity funding that was just halved
Decision 6(d) elevates 'librarians, arts, TAG' to protected status 'because they are the storefront.' === 14. [FATAL] ? on D6: The payback arithmetic is backwards: recovered students earn $0 in year one under Extended ADMw
The plan cites the higher-of rule as the reason 'recovery pays almost immediately.' It works exactly the other way in a declining district. Oregon funds on Extended ADMw — the higher of current or prior year — and PPS is already being paid on the prior-year count: the district's own budget book shows funded ADMw of 51,390 against actual enrollment falling from 42,467 to 41,661 [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p.~35, 'Extended ADMw... higher of their current or prior year's enrollment']. That means a marginal recovered kindergartner adds zero formula revenue until recovery exceeds the annual decline of roughly 800 students; the claim that 100 recovered kindergartners are '$1.2M in year one' is arithmetically false, and I will say so at the board table with the book open. The '$12,000-13,000 in formula and outside revenue' also overstates the margin: the local option levy is a property tax that does not rise by one dollar when a student enrolls, and compression already takes $53.4M off those lines [tscc-annual-report-2024-25-general p50]. The long-run logic — recovered cohorts propping up every future year's count — is sound, but the plan leads with the one version of the math a finance office can kill in ninety seconds, and once one number dies in public, the board discounts all of them.
FIX: Rewrite the payback honestly: model marginal revenue against the Extended ADMw baseline, showing zero-to-small returns in years 1-2 and the real compounding thereafter, and drop levy dollars from the per-student figure. The decision itself survives on the corrected math; the current arithmetic does not.

=== 15. [FATAL] ? on D7: The calendar the plan wants to 'rebuild' is a bargained document, and the fragmentation is the professional development and planning time we negotiated
Decision 7(b) proposes rebuilding the calendar by board decision at a cost of 'bargaining attention, not dollars.' Article 6.8 gives the Association a formal role in calendar recommendations, requires PD and planning days to be set in the calendar before the prior year ends [§6.8.2], and bars any change that increases aggregate workdays without Association consent [§6.8.1]; the non-five-day weeks the plan counts as 'fragmentation' — 16 of 38 [cbrc-2025-26-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p7-8] — are substantially the PD days, planning days, grading days, and conference structure of Articles 6.3 and 6.7, won in bargaining because educator preparation time is instruction. 'Reducing fragmentation' therefore means either converting educator work time into student-contact time (a workload increase we will price) or adding days (which §6.8.1 forbids without our consent). The plan's cost line is not an estimate, it is a hope: any real calendar restructuring lands in successor bargaining with a dollar figure attached, and pretending otherwise sets the board up to promise the public an attendance fix it cannot deliver alone. We are not opposed to calendar coherence — our members hate fragmented weeks too — but a plan that budgets zero for it has not talked to anyone who has ever bargained a calendar.
FIX: Reframe 7(b) as a joint labor-management calendar redesign to be proposed in successor bargaining, with an honest cost range (compensated time, not zero) and PAT named as co-author of the design. Drop the implication that the board can adopt it by goal-setting.

=== 16. [FATAL] ? on D7: The calendar motion cannot be lawfully executed by the board — it is a mandatory subject, mid-status-quo, with an expired PAT contract
Decision 7(b) directs the board to 'rebuild the calendar to reduce fragmentation' as though the calendar were the board's to rebuild. It is not. The work-year structure that produces the 16-of-38 fragmentation the plan cites is negotiated text: Article 6.8 of the PAT agreement gives the Association a formal role in calendar-setting, forbids any aggregate increase in workdays without Association consent (cba-pat-2023-2026.txt, 6.8.1), fixes PD and planning days into the calendar before the prior year ends (6.8.2), and blacklists specific dates (6.8.3.1); the counts and placements of PD days, early releases, conference days, and grading days — the very sources of fragmentation — are all Article 6 terms. Worse, the agreement expired June 30, 2026 (Article 31.1), successor bargaining opened no later than January 2026 (Article 28.3), and the contract contains express status-quo carryover language (8.3.1, 8.4.1); under PECBA, unilateral change to a mandatory subject during status quo is an unfair labor practice under ORS 243.672(1)(e), and hours and work-year structure are squarely mandatory under ORS 243.650(7). A board resolution 'rebuilding' the calendar in the plan's first budget cycle is either an empty aspiration or a ULP charge filed within the month, and announcing the district's calendar position by public resolution on the eve of successor negotiations hands PAT the chip for free. The plan's own authority line — 'Calendar within board/labor purview' — concedes the problem in four words and then ignores it in the timeline.
FIX: Recast 7(b) in two lawful pieces: (i) a board goal directing the district's bargaining team to prioritize calendar consolidation in the successor agreement, with public reporting on whether it was achieved; and (ii) whatever consolidation the district can do unilaterally under its retained 6.8.1 authority, identified by counsel first. Move the expected landing date to the successor contract's first full year, not FY2027-28.

=== 17. [FATAL] ? on overall: This plan directs unilateral action on mandatory bargaining subjects in the middle of open successor negotiations
The PAT contract expired June 30, 2026 [cba-pat-2023-2026 §31.1: 'effective the 1st day of July 2023 and shall continue through June 30, 2026'], which means the board this plan addresses in September 2026 is at the successor bargaining table with us right now. Decision 4 caps instructional-coach FTE (bargaining-unit positions), targets overload pay (Article 8 compensation we struck for), and Decision 7 'rebuilds the calendar' — every one of these is a mandatory or contractually governed subject under PECBA and the agreement itself, and Article 8.3.1 explicitly places overload and class-size language in a status quo period. A board resolution adopting these as unilateral policy during open negotiations is either a regressive bargaining position laundered through a think-piece or an invitation to an unfair labor practice charge; either way it converts a 'transparency plan' into a management proposal we will treat as one at the table. The plan's authority lines — 'fully within board budget authority,' 'calendar within board/labor purview' — are written to obscure this, and a board member who votes these motions believing they are free actions has been misled about what they cost. We beat this district over exactly this kind of unilateralism in November 2023, and the settlement's terms are the proof of how that ends.

=== 43. [SERIOUS] ? on D8: Compression is in the Constitution, and the SPED cap is a fixed pie
