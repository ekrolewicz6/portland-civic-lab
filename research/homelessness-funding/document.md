## Funding Portland's homelessness response with federal dollars instead of local ones, without losing either

*Portland Civic Lab · September 2026 · Working draft for review*

*Every figure below traces to a public document listed in [sources.md](sources.md), cited as [id]. Judgments are ours and labeled. What we could not verify is in section 8.*

---

The cost-of-inaction section of the homelessness deep dive makes a claim that is true and unsatisfying: leaving a chronically homeless person on the street costs the public roughly twice what housing them costs, and most of the saving lands on Medicaid rather than on the city or county. A reader is left with no local reason to act and no federal mechanism to act through. This memo answers the question that follows: **how does Portland fund what it needs with dollars from the payer who saves, and how do the city, the three counties, Metro, the state, and the health plans split the work so the local burden falls while outcomes improve?**

The short answer has four parts.

1. **Identify the benefit and payer.** Medicaid coverage requires the relevant authority, eligible person, qualified provider and covered service. A locally funded service is not automatically Medicaid-billable, and not every displaced local dollar becomes savings.
2. **Model capitation and risk correctly.** Federal and state funding reaches OHA; OHA pays predetermined monthly CCO amounts. Lower claims may change plan finances under risk arrangements and later rates. There is no fixed federal/CCO split of each avoided claim.
3. **Obtain an actual reinvestment commitment.** SHARE designations depend on applicable financial conditions and spending rules, community priorities and partnerships. Housing is a priority; the County’s pipeline is not automatically the beneficiary.
4. **Fund the complete service.** Construction, licensing, staffing, clinical operations, housing and transition support need separate commitments. The Council’s proposed break-even inpatient reimbursement must specify costs, payment authority, payer and recurring budget.

## 1. The constraint ledger

Every dollar in the system carries a rule about who can spend it on what. Most of the argument about homelessness funding is settled by these rules, not by anyone in a council chamber.

| Dollar | Who controls it | Can pay for | Cannot pay for | Trajectory |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Medicaid, regular match** | OHA, via CCOs | Clinical services, case management, tenancy supports where a state plan or waiver authorizes them | Rent (outside the waiver), capital, shelter operations | Federal share 58.18% in FY2027 [kff-fmap]. H.R. 1 restricts provider taxes and state-directed payments; OHA projects at least $1B in cuts in 2027–29 [or-hearing] |
| **Medicaid, expansion adults** | Same | Same | Same | 90% federal share by statute. This is the population work requirements target from January 1, 2027 [oha-federal-changes] |
| **1115 waiver HRSN housing benefit** | OHA; CareOregon administers for all Health Share members [healthshare-hrsn] | Up to 6 months rent and utilities, one time per household; home modifications; tenancy support | Ongoing rent, capital | Live since November 1, 2024. Waiver ends September 30, 2027. CMS rescinded HRSN guidance March 4, 2025; renewals now case by case [cms-rescission] |
| **CCO SHARE reinvestment** | Each CCO, under OAR 410-141-3735 | Anything addressing social determinants, **including capital**; a portion must go to housing | Must meet SHARE qualifying-expenditure and spending requirements | Historical draft formula, to be checked against 2026 guidance: 0% to 20% of three-year average adjusted net income on a sliding scale by risk-based capital, or 10% of dividends, whichever is greater [share-guidance]. CCOs statewide put $104M into housing through SHARE and flexible services 2018–2024 [oha-housing-medicaid] |
| **CCO flexible services** | Each CCO | Member-level short-term rent, deposits, AC units, legal help to keep housing; community-level grants | **Capital** [oha-housing-medicaid] | Ongoing; HRSN must be used first if the member is eligible |
| **HUD Continuum of Care** | Local CoC board; HUD | Permanent supportive housing, rapid rehousing, coordinated entry | Shelter operations at scale, capital | OR-501 received $37.7M in FY2024 [hud-coc-or]. Two successive HUD attempts to cap PSH and divert a third of funds were vacated by a federal court on June 30 and August 7, 2026; FY2026 applications are not being accepted while HUD weighs appeal [naco-fy26]. The President's FY2027 budget proposes eliminating CoC into a $4B ESG block; the House committee bill keeps CoC with a cut [crs-hud-fy27] |
| **Metro SHS tax** | Metro, allocated to three counties | Services, rent assistance, shelter, outreach; **not capital** under the 2020 measure | Affordable housing construction (Metro wants to change this) | $325M FY2025, forecast $351M FY2026 including a $40M one-time bump, with "pronounced weakness" in Multnomah County's economy behind it [ww-shs-forecast]. Expires 2030. Thresholds now inflation-indexed. Metro dropped a November 2026 ballot measure and is targeting extension to 2050 plus capital eligibility [kgw-metro-measure] |
| **Multnomah County general fund and HSD** | County board | Anything | Nothing categorical; the constraint is size | Homeless Services Department falls from $334M to $247M in FY2027 [katu-hsd], a $67M structural gap; 605 adult shelter units and 90 family vouchers close; 158 positions cut [multco-fy27] |
| **City of Portland** | City council | Shelter, outreach, cleanup, Portland Street Response | Nothing categorical | City keeps the $31M it used to send the county and cuts shelter 31% to $40M and outreach 37% to $2.8M [portland-fy27] |
| **State general fund** | Legislature | Shelter (HB 3644 regional program, $204M), rehousing, eviction prevention | Nothing categorical | 2025–27: $217.9M to maintain shelters, $204M for the statewide shelter program with regional coordinators; eviction prevention funded at $33.6M against a $173M request [streetroots-2025] |

Three things fall out of the ledger.

**The local money is shrinking on every line at once.** County, city, and Metro are all contracting in the same fiscal year, and the county's cut is a structural gap, not a one-year dip. Anyone proposing to "just fund it locally" is proposing it into a hole.

**The federal money that exists is legally shaped.** Medicaid can pay for tenancy supports indefinitely and rent for six months. It cannot pay for a building or a shelter bed. CoC can pay for permanent housing subsidies but not shelter. So the question is never "federal or local." It is which cost belongs to which payer, and whether local dollars are currently sitting on costs a federal payer would carry.

**Every federal door has a closing date.** The waiver: September 2027. Work requirements: January 2027. CoC: a court fight the administration may appeal, then a 2027 appropriation. The plan has to be sequenced against those dates, not against a wish list.

## 2. Where the street's cost lands, and who captures the saving

The deep dive's $40,000-per-year street figure is a national central estimate. The payer split matters more than the total, because the payer who saves is the one who can be asked to pay.

The best-documented local breakdown is Los Angeles County's Housing for Health program, evaluated by RAND. County service costs per participant fell from $38,146 in the year before housing to $15,358 in the year after; housing cost $15,288; the program returned $1.20 for every dollar spent in year one. Emergency visits fell 68%, inpatient stays 77%. The county health department alone saw about $20M less spending in the first year [rand-h4h]. The decisive design fact: **the county health department funded the housing out of its own budget, because it was the payer that saved.**

Portland ran the same experiment on a smaller scale. In 2016 six health systems put $21.5M into Central City Concern to build roughly 380 units. Providence's outcomes center measured 1,600 Medicaid members across 145 affordable properties: emergency visits fell 18%, inpatient care 15%, primary care rose 20%, and Medicaid spending per resident dropped 12% [providence-core]. The hospitals paid because uncompensated care is their cost.

Denver's social impact bond ran the justice-system version: 250 frequent jail users, $8.6M in private capital, 86% still housed at one year and 81% at two, annual service costs $6,876 lower than the $25,554 control-group cost, and the city repaid investors $9.6M on outcomes [urban-denver]. The city paid because jail days are its cost.

North Carolina's Medicaid pilots are the cautionary tale. Over 31,000 people, $164 per member per month in reduced Medicaid spending, verified by an independent evaluator, and CMS authorization through 2029. The legislature declined to fund the state share after July 1, 2025 and the program suspended anyway [nc-hop]. **A proven saving to the federal payer did not protect a program from a state budget.** Minnesota's state-plan housing benefit, once the model for durability, was terminated October 31, 2025 after provider growth and spending outran oversight [mn-hss].

The lesson for Portland's payer map:

- **Federal Medicaid and OHA** finance eligible expenditures, including capitation. Budget effects depend on rates, eligible spending and appropriations; an avoided claim does not automatically return its federal matching share.
- **CCOs and health plans** may experience lower claims under their contractual risk arrangements. SHARE is conditional reinvestment, not a fixed return of savings or an automatic County earmark.
- **Hospitals** may experience changes to costs, uncompensated care and revenue, which must be analyzed by payer and service.
- **County and City** may avoid variable costs or free capacity; larger cash savings require actual budget changes. Fewer episodes do not automatically release fixed facility or staffing costs.

Ask for the service-level operating costs, actual payment, risk arrangements and investment commitment before claiming that housing or clinical care pays for itself.

## 3. Four doors to federal dollars, ranked by durability

**Door 1. Confirm covered services.** Audit each service against Oregon benefit authority, eligibility, provider qualifications, authorization, rates and actual payments. Washington’s experience is a comparison, not proof of Oregon coverage. Local funds may still be needed for uncovered services, operations and households.

**Door 2. Secure a SHARE commitment.** Request the applicable financial calculations, approved spending plans, community-priority alignment and project commitments. An eligible capital project may qualify, but neither its amount nor the County’s role follows automatically from a health-plan claim reduction.

**Door 3. Deliver eligible rent benefits.** Budget intake, outreach, documentation and payment follow-through. These activities need workers and operating funds. Measure paid assistance and subsequent housing stability, with unknown follow-up separate. Confirm current waiver eligibility and renewal status when acting.

**Door 4. Defend the CoC and prepare for its loss.** *Least controllable.* The $37.7M OR-501 grant is the region's permanent supportive housing backbone. Two court wins in 2026 preserved it for now. The FY2027 budget request would abolish it. The regional posture should be two-track: participate in the litigation coalition through the state, and model the region's exposure if CoC becomes a smaller ESG block grant that cannot fund PSH subsidies.

## 4. The regional compact: who does what

The city–county intergovernmental agreement is effectively dissolved for FY2027: the city kept its $31M and is providing shelter directly, the county closed 605 shelter units, and the two governments dispute the by-name count itself [kgw-mayor-budget] [opb-data-fight]. The Homelessness Response Action Plan steering committee still exists with five voting members: the county chair, one county commissioner, the mayor, one city councilor, and Gresham's mayor [multco-hrap]. That committee is the only table where a compact could be signed.

A compact that lowers every party's burden assigns each cost to its natural payer:

| Cost | Natural payer | Instrument |
|---|---|---|
| Services in supportive housing (tenancy support, case management, behavioral health) | Medicaid via CCO | Provider billing; Health Share contract amendment; HRSN for eligible members |
| Six-month rent to prevent an eviction | Medicaid via HRSN | Fix the intake pipe; county and city outreach teams as referral sources |
| Rent beyond six months | Metro SHS, HUD CoC | SHS rent assistance; CoC PSH subsidies |
| Capital for supportive housing | Metro bond, state, CCO SHARE, hospital co-investment | SHARE capital designation; a second Housing is Health round |
| Shelter operations | City, state HB 3644 program | State regional coordinator allocation; city shelter budget |
| Treatment beds | OHA behavioral health investment programs, Medicaid | State capital; Medicaid billing for operations |
| Jail and hospital discharge bridges | County jail, hospitals, CCO | Discharge-to-street ban (the flow model's cheapest lever); hospital-funded medical respite (SHARE has funded this elsewhere in Oregon) |
| Outreach, cleanup, Portland Street Response | City | City general fund; this is the local cost that is genuinely local |

Three rules make it work across five governments and three counties.

**One funnel, one denominator.** The city–county data dispute is not a side issue. No payer will co-fund against a number the two governments do not agree on. The compact's first deliverable is a shared by-name list and a shared placement funnel (referral, engagement, treatment admission, housing exit, retention), which is also what the deep dive's plan step 4 calls for.

**Clackamas and Washington counties are not passengers.** SHS sends them $40M to $60M a year each [opb-shs-2025], and Health Share covers all three counties. A tri-county Medicaid billing standard and a single CCO co-funding agreement are cheaper to negotiate once than three times.

**The state's regional coordinators are the lever for HB 3644 money.** The 2025 shelter program routes $204M through regional coordinators appointed by OHCS [streetroots-2025]. A region that shows up with a compact and a funnel gets allocated differently from one that shows up with a dispute.

## 5. The sequence

Ordered by deadline, not by size.

**Now through December 2026.**
- County and city jointly inventory every contract that pays for a Medicaid-billable service with local dollars. This is the denominator for Door 1.
- Health Share is asked, in writing, for its SHARE housing designation for 2024–2026 by project and county, and for a co-funding agreement that names the county pipeline as its vehicle.
- Fix HRSN intake: a single referral workflow from city and county outreach teams into CareOregon's HRSN unit, with case numbers.
- Records request to OHA for the draft 2027 waiver renewal and any HRSN continuation language.

**January 2027.** Work requirements and six-month renewals begin. People in substance-use treatment and people recently released from incarceration are exempt [orpca-hr1]; outreach and jail-release teams should be enrolling and documenting exemptions, because coverage loss can interrupt Medicaid supports. HUD and local safety-net eligibility follow separate rules.

**2027 legislative session.** The state ask is not more shelter money. It is (a) eviction prevention restored toward the $173M the governor requested, structured to draw HRSN match where members are eligible, and (b) a statutory backstop for tenancy-support billing if the waiver's HRSN authority lapses, so the services side survives on the state plan.

**By September 30, 2027.** Waiver renewal decision. Oregon should file with housing in it and with the CCO co-funding agreements as evidence of local match commitment.

**2027–2028.** Metro's SHS extension measure. The region's position should be that SHS pays for what federal dollars cannot (rent past six months, shelter, and, if the measure allows it, capital), and that its rent-assistance line is explicitly braided with HRSN so the same household is not funded twice.

**2030.** SHS expires. If the sequence above holds, the services layer is on Medicaid, capital is on bond and SHARE, and SHS is buying rent and shelter. If it does not, the region is asking voters to renew a tax to pay for services a federal payer would have carried.
