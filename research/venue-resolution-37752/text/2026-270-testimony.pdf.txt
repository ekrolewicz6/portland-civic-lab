Support To the folks of the Portland City Council, I'm writing in support of the new theater at PSU to serve the current function
of the Keller, and allow for its reimagining as an 1800 seat venue. As someone who has been making theater in
Portland for two decades, from Shakespeare in shoebox venues to full-scale Broadway productions at the Keller
Auditorium, I can attest to the vibrant interest that our city holds for the performing arts. The plan under discussion
would allow us to maintain our momentum as a top tier arts destination while growing our capacity for more rich,
diverse, and entertaining public performance.
This is the best option to support workers, fuel economic growth, and serve the community. The public theaters of
Portland are an institution of public good, and need to be protected by the City. Our theaters enrich our communities,
create stable, fulfilling jobs, and inspire us to make our city better. The arts are deeply at risk in these uncertain times,
and the Portland State University Performing Arts Center / Keller Auditorium renovation project provides the best plan
for preserving Broadway, opera, and ballet for future generations.
Thank you for your time.

Support Good morning fellow Portlanders,
I am writing in support of agenda item 2026-270, which seeks approval for a new Broadway-capable venue as part of
the proposed Performing Arts + Culture Center at Portland State University.
I live in Portland and am a full time arts worker. I have worked in wardrobe, wigs, and makeup for more than 30 years
and am a member of IATSE Local 28, the theatrical and stage workers' union.In that time I have seen many changes in
the arts ecosystem here, and the most recent years have been some of the roughest.
I believe that the proposed Arts and Culture Center at PSU is an excellent remedy to several ongoing problems with
our aging venues, improve accessibility to patrons from all over the city, and provide equitable access to suitable
performance spaces for some of our smaller local nonprofit performing arts organizations. Once built, it would preserve
and even create more living wage jobs and while operating, it would allow the Keller Auditorium to be renovated or
reimagined without disruption of employment or of the significant revenue from the touring shows, concerts, and other
productions that currently come to the Keller.
Contrary to what some reports have stated, Portland  is more than capable of supporting a new Arts Center. More
urgently, it is needed as the aging venues in town are badly in need of updating for safety and accessibility, and this
cannot be put off much longer. Portland also cannot afford a 3 to 5 year cessation of the touring shows and concerts
that come through the Keller. Much of that revenue would simply disappear in the gap as would too many living wage
jobs. That cost is too high.
I urge you to support this agenda itemand vote to advance this plan for a new Arts and Culture Center at PSU.
Thank you for your time and attention,
Bonnie

Kate Mura Support Two years ago, I submitted an opinion piece to Oregon ArtsWatch on remembering stagehands when contemplating
the fate of the Keller Auditorium. What I wrote then remains true now: “Broadway in Portland must always have a good,
appropriate home. Just ask a stagehand."
Reading through Hunden Partner's feasibility study, I agree that focusing on maximizing Broadway content and
maintaining theatre-style commercial content, while also being an accessible home to resident companies and
nonprofits, is important. The PSU construction and Keller renovation plan provides this, including a full performing arts
campus and support amenities achievable at PSU, no downtime in hosting large scale shows (which support the other
Portland'5 venues), as well as opportunities for audience and subscription growth.
I am concerned by a gap in the same Hunden Partner's feasibility study, which primarily focused on audiences, and
existing rentals, while ignoring another revenue driver: A second Broadway capacity house would be a prime location
for upcoming Broadway tours to rehearse and prepare, and existing tours to repair and update equipment, as we were
able to do for The Lion King. Numerous tours have wished they could prep their shows in Portland due to our highly
skilled stagehands and lack of sales tax. Producers can save money and time working here while spending money
locally, and it is unfortunate that formal research on this subject seems not to have happened, either via contacting
tours or speaking with local stagehands. This study also did not investigate how many tours have been unable to come
to Portland due to the limited availability of the Keller.
Thank you for your kind attention.
Please support & advance the proposed Performing Arts + Culture Center at Portland State University.
Sincerely,
Kate Mura
Theatre professional and constituent in 97215
Full opinion piece is here:
https://www.orartswatch.org/opinion-when-deciding-the-fate-of-keller-auditorium-think-of-the-people-who-work-there/

Carl M Halvorson Support Chair Pirtle-Guiney and Members of the Committee on City Life:
For the record, my name is Carl Halvorson. I am testifying in support of the resolution to advance a new Broadway-
capable venue at PSU.
After a long career performing as tenor soloist around the world in many of the greatest concert halls and opera
houses, I have long bemoaned the Keller Auditorium. In fact, I’ve been going to it since it was remodeled in the 60s,
I’ve sung on its stage numerous times with Portland Opera, and I’ve always seen it as a cultural blight on our city. I
have so often seen the disappointed looks on guest artists’ faces when they first work on the Keller stage - to say
nothing of the audience members straining to see and hear them!
The flaws with the hall are too numerous to mention here, and I mean flaws that would not be addressed in original
proposed remodel. Very poor acoustics, football field-length sight lines, and that tiny 200’x200’ lot are just a few. It’s no
surprise to me that the experts have all solidly backed the PSU proposal.
We would finally have a normally-shaped hall purpose-built with
current advanced acoustical expertise, boxes and balconies that are closer to the stage and with improved sight lines,
state-of-the-art equipment in a generous backstage with excellent loading docks, a full-sized rehearsal stage (this is so
needed), and much more.
I believe that making the Keller into a much smaller hall would mitigate its inherent flaws, some left over from the old
Auditorium. I note that Avery Fisher Hall, where I sang many times, has finally become a wonderful hall after the New
York Philharmonic changed its interior shape in a remodel. Losing that top balcony and narrowing the hall would vastly
improve the Keller.
I have witnessed that all major American cities have heavily invested in wonderful performing arts complexes, with
many halls of different sizes. After sixty years, Portland finally deserves the same.
I thank you very much for your service to our city.

From the Desk of Rob Nosse, State Representative 
1712 SE 47th Avenue, Portland, Oregon 97215 
 
July 26, 2026 
 
Chair Pirtle-Guiney and Members of the City Life Committee:  
 
As one of the leader of the Arts & Culture Caucus in the Oregon Legislatures and as a 
long-time supporter of the Portland arts community, I am writing to urge you to support 
the resolution to “Accept the Future of Large-Scale Performing Arts recommendations 
to advance a new Broadway-capable venue as part of the proposed Performing Arts + 
Culture Center at Portland State University, and at the same time initiate planning for 
the future of the Keller Auditorium.”  
 
During COVID, the arts community experienced unprecedented challenges. Venues went 
dark, backstage crews and creative workers faced extended hardship due to loss of 
work, and our cultural ecosystem suffered many financial shocks. As we work to 
revitalize downtown Portland, we cannot afford to take steps backward or put our 
region’s primary performing arts engine on ice. 
 
Developing a new Broadway-capable venue at Portland State University is the way to 
go. It is the clearest, most economically responsible path forward for our city. A 
purpose-built venue integrated with an urban university opens doors for local, 
emerging, and culturally diverse arts groups that have historically lacked affordable 
access to world-class facilities.  
 
Portland stands at a pivotal moment. With momentum building across public leadership, 
philanthropic partners, and community stakeholders, we have an incredible opportunity 
to anchor our city's creative identity for the next century.

Keller Testimony 
 
My name is Jan Castle, I am a native Portlander, and as of one year ago, a 
downtown resident. I live in the South Auditorium District very close to the Keller  
Auditorium and Fountain. 
 
I am unable to address you personally because of illness, but if I were, I would 
speak from the heart about the profound experience I had yesterday re-reading 
the Minority Report to the Ex-Officio Table Recommendation of June 3, 2026, 
which speaks to the recommendations made by Deputy City Administrator Donnie 
Oliveira.  
 
What really got through to me in this reading was how severely this two-venue 
option would weaken the financial underpinnings of the entire performing arts 
ecosystem, perhaps permanently.  It would also double or triple the amount the 
City would have to spend on subsidizing the P5 complex. 
 
You are all aware that the Keller generates a surplus of $1.2 million a year, a 
million of which it contributes to the City’s subsidization of the P5 complex, 
reducing the City’s expenditure from $5 million to $4 million.  
 
If a new venue at PSU takes the Broadway series and the Keller retains the opera, 
ballet and White Bird series, as is recommended in the latest proposal, the 
performances will be spread too thin for either venue to be able to cover their 
expenses. Even if they were all at one or the other of these venues, the 
competition from the other venue would weaken their take enough that they still 
wouldn’t be profitable.  The revenue the Keller generates only works at one 
venue.  Competition from another venue destroys the profit, eliminating the extra 
million the City has to spend supporting the smaller P5 venues. 
 
While either venue would scramble to generate new business, comments in both 
Hunden reports and the article Mike Rogoway wrote for the Oregonian both cast 
doubt on those efforts’ ability to fully fill the gap. 

--- PDF PAGE 10 ---
I see no documented evidence that Portland needs a 1200-1800 seat venue. To 
the contrary, the Hunden Partners Dec. 2025 Venue User Survey showed a need 
for 300-600 and 500-1400 seat venues. No respondent asked for a 1200-1800 seat 
venue. 
 
I appreciate that you are very busy and must rely on staff to bring you information 
to consider.  Please note that all of the information you have been provided 
supports the venue at PSU. There has been no attempt to provide you with 
anything that would support the renovation of the Keller and some creative 
thinking about how to provide space for Broadway productions while it is under 
construction.  Unless you want your legacy to be the dismantling of the successful 
and low cost support of the performing arts ecosystem and the increase of the 
City’s subsidization, I’m afraid you are going to have to do your own homework.  I 
would recommend that you review two documents, which hopefully you have 
already seen. One is the Keller Feasibility Study and the other is the Minority 
Report to the Ex-Officio Table Recommendation of June 3, 2026.  
 
The real problem to be solved is where to house Broadway productions during a 
Keller renovation.  Please, spend money on that. Do not spend any more money 
on studies to justify something that makes no sense.

Chair Pirtle Guiney and City Life Committee Members, 
 
I am a recently retired Intel principal engineer who oversaw seismic design and construction for facilities 
around the world and a former chair of the Oregon Seismic Safety Commission (OSSPAC). Throughout 
my career, I helped organizations make long-term infrastructure decisions that balanced safety, risk, and 
value. 
 
I am writing because seismic safety should remain at the center of the discussion about Keller 
Auditorium. 
 
The Keller was built in 1917, almost 100 years before modern seismic standards existed. Unreinforced 
masonry buildings like the Keller are some of the most dangerous buildings in the City’s inventory and 
given that there is a 37 percent chance of a major earthquake on the Cascadia Subduction Zone in the 
next 50 years, there is no time to lose. 
 
The City has rightly been searching for a long-term solution to these challenges. What concerns me is 
the recent suggestion that an "affordable fix" is spending $10-$15 million on deferred maintenance of 
the current Keller facility. Maintaining the Keller is important, but maintenance alone does not solve the 
underlying seismic challenges that have driven this conversation for years. Deferred maintenance is not 
a seismic retrofit. 
 
At the same time, our region has been presented with a significant opportunity through Portland State 
University's proposed Performing Arts + Culture Center. The City can be a partner in a purpose-built 
performing arts complex designed to modern standards and built for the next century. 
 
There are many cultural benefits to this proposal that others are better positioned to discuss. For me, 
one advantage rises above the rest: it solves the long-term seismic challenge the City has struggled to 
address for years. It also offers a level of predictability that major renovations rarely can. New 
construction on a large, unconstrained site provides far greater certainty around costs, schedule, and 
outcomes than work on a century-old building, where major surprises often emerge only after 
construction begins. 
 
This is not a choice between maintaining the Keller and building the Center. The Keller can continue 
serving Portland while the Center is developed and Broadway performances continue uninterrupted. 
The Keller site's long-term future should be determined through a City-led public process that centers on 
both civic benefit and seismic concerns. 
 
I urge the Committee to prioritize the safety of Portland residents and support the Performing Arts + 
Culture Center which will not only create an exceptional cultural asset but will be resilient to future 
devastating seismic events in our region. 
 
Thank you for your consideration and leadership, 
 
Jeff Soulages

A Vision for Transformative Impact 
The steering committee’s unanimous recommendation sets forth a cohesive vision for the 
transformation of performing arts in Portland. We support the pairing of a bold, creative 
investment in the new Portland State University Performing Arts + Culture Center along with 
civic stewardship and reinvention of the Keller Auditorium and/or site. Together, the investments 
will protect the economic engine of Broadway touring productions, support nonprofit arts 
organizations, strengthen the academic mission of Portland State University (PSU) and 
revitalize a key area of downtown Portland that includes the historic Ira Keller Fountain and 
Portland Open Space Sequence. By acting decisively now, the City can seize a 
once‑in‑a‑generation opportunity to build a performing arts ecosystem that is more resilient, 
inclusive, and vibrant. 
 
Recommendation 
Build a new Broadway‑capable venue as part of the proposed Performing Arts + Culture Center 
at Portland State University (PSU). Simultaneously, create an actionable plan to reimagine the 
Keller Auditorium and/or site based on Portland’s historic, cultural, and civic values in 
accordance with passionate public support expressed by the community.  
This unanimous committee recommendation supports a forward-thinking vision that expands the 
arts ecosystem and redefines Portland’s place on the nation’s cultural map for generations to 
come. 
  
A New Broadway‑Capable Venue at PSU 
After comprehensive review, the steering committee’s recommended approach avoids the 
catastrophic economic and employment impacts that would accompany a multi‑year Keller 

--- PDF PAGE 47 ---
Auditorium shutdown—estimated at two years of closure and 3–5 years without touring 
Broadway shows. These losses would be an extremely difficult recovery for Portland’s arts 
ecosystem and downtown businesses. 
Although considered by the committee, a temporary venue is not a viable option if the Keller is 
renovated to continue as a Broadway-capable facility. There are no existing venues in Portland 
or proven temporary venue models that could meet the needs of Broadway‑scale seating 
capacity, audience experience, and technical requirements. 
In contrast, PSU’s proposed complex delivers substantial immediate and long-term advantages. 
PSU already holds $157.6 million in committed funding from the State of Oregon, Prosper 
Portland, and private donors, with potential for further philanthropic investment due to the 
project’s scale and ambition. It will also leverage substantial private investment. The project 
meets the city’s need for a large modern auditorium, while enhancing the university’s academic 
mission and advancing training in all aspects of the performing arts. The new complex will also 
provide essential affordable rehearsal and performance space for small arts organizations—an 
ongoing gap in the city’s arts infrastructure. PSU has a stated intent to raise funds for this 
supportive role and for long term maintenance.  
The Essential Future of the Keller Auditorium 
The committee emphasizes in the strongest possible terms that the City must not allow the 
Keller Auditorium to sit unused or deteriorate but rather be reimagined and continue to provide 
public benefit. Its location, cultural significance, and relationship to the Keller Fountain make it a 
uniquely valuable civic asset. The site links to the existing cultural institutions and open spaces 
along the Broadway-South Park Blocks corridor and to new destinations such as the James 
Beard Public Market and Performing Arts + Culture Center. Furthermore, given its seismic 
condition, investment is mandatory even if it is not redeveloped as a large-scale theater. 
Multiple ideas have emerged from community and stakeholder feedback. While the steering 
committee does not endorse a single option, it unanimously recommends that the City begin an 
inclusive and immediate process to explore a major public space that honors the arts, the 
cultural landscape, and has public benefits. That process should include a committee to study 
options and budget, and community engagement to explore future concepts for the Keller 
Auditorium and/or site. The goal is to ensure that a plan is ready to implement when the new 
PSU theater opens.

As a longtime resident of the Halprin historic neighborhood, I support the efforts being made to 
ﬁnd a more compatible use to complement the iconic Keller Fountain. But I question why the

To expend years of effort and millions of City dollars to salvage a 100-year old, poorly-functioning 
facility, that was never intended for its current use, would miss two, incredible opportunities: to 
revitalize the Halprin historic neighborhood and to create a world-class performance center within 
that neighborhood. 
The problem with the Keller Auditorium is not simply about the auditorium space—the public side 
of the facility—which admittedly could be tweaked for better sightlines and various other nominal 
improvements. The problem is about the back of house, or rather the lack thereof. 
For contemporary music theater—complex productions such as opera and musicals—roughly the 
same amount of space is required back of house as in front of the stage. Keller’s vaudeville era 
roots simply cannot provide the storage and maneuverability for contemporary theater, nor even 
decent facilities for the hundreds of performers and production staff that make up a typical behind 
the scenes musical production. (See the diagram to the left comparing the size of Keller 
Auditorium in relation to two other more contemporary facilities.) 
Besides its cramped back of house, the other problem with the Keller is that it is used for one 
purpose only—large-scale productions. It has no other capability. For the remainder of the time it is 
a dark and lifeless hulk. The auditorium is used for roughly 28% of the year, but only for about four 
hours per day, and then mostly at night—400 hours of use out of 8760 hours per year—4.5% of the 
year. For the remainder, the lights are off. 
For these reasons alone the PSU proposal is a breath of fresh air. Not only because of more 
realistic and humane back of house provisions, and a state of the art auditorium, but because it 
proposes varied auditorium sizes and more diverse uses that will allow other types of performance
—chamber music and dance—and bring constant activity to the facility. 
But, there is yet another signiﬁcant reason to favor the PSU proposal—it would ﬁnally resolve a 
long-standing urban ﬂaw within the historic neighborhood. The site proposed for the 
performance center was part of the same urban renewal district (created around 1960) as the 
remainder of the “South Auditorium” neighborhood. But because the site was developed as a 
dreary motel with an expansive parking lot (ironically, both considered state-of-the-art at the time) 
the site has been neglected, as though beyond the urban limits of the city.  
The PSU proposal would ﬁnally anchor the south boundary of the district just as the the Keller 
Fountain and its new complement anchor the north boundary. This would provide better deﬁnition 
to the historic district and bring some life to an attractive, but mostly lifeless neighborhood. (See 
the neighborhood diagram to the left locating Keller auditorium and the proposed PSU facility.) 
Back to the search for a Keller Auditorium replacement, the handsome renderings prepared by 
Hennebery Eddy best capture the spirit of what the new Keller ought to be—a lively place used 
throughout the day and throughout the year, a destination for the entire community. 
As a footnote, the Metropolitan Opera produced a surprisingly (shockingly?) candid documentary
—The Opera House—about its own painful (and often vituperative) process switching from the old 
Metropolitan Opera House to Lincoln Center. It captures the same contrary forces as with the 
Keller, those who emphasize nostalgic historicism versus an emphasis on achieving the best 
performance facilities. The latter won out in New York. Let’s hope the same happens in Portland.
This dazzling west facing facade is exactly what the Keller Fountain and 
downtown Portland need. But a better purpose needs to be found for the 
building behind the facade that will promote this kind of vitality all day long 
and throughout the year. 
Equally important is the treatment of the south elevation facing the historic 
district, which currently is just a blank wall with emergency doors, thereby 
adding to the dismal, vehicle-oriented character of SW Market.

The resolution before you today is a word salad of mind-numbing proportions.  May I suggest that you

Council resolves and commitments: 
In summary, Council continues to support PSU’s planning and pre-development efforts BUT

center until substantially more research, analysis and planning is completed and presented 
for review and approval.   
Here are the details: 
1. support for advancing planning and pre-development for a potential new City-owned

B.1. Borrowing cost increases 
 
In recent news, we see headlines like this:

Municipal bond yields, as always, are subject to ﬂuctuations in the municipal bond market.  But this 
time is diƯerent.  Now, inevitably, the bond market is paying attention to a persistent upward trend 
in the inﬂation rate.  Bond yields have doubled from their COVID low (<2.5% in 2022) to about 5%.  
Keep in mind that a single percentage point, applied to a $450 million bond, equals a $4.5 million 
increase in annual debt service.  That’s a line item in the city’s overstressed budget or, if ﬁnanced 
through a bond measure, everyone’s household budget. 
 
And that’s far from the worst-case scenario.  We’ve encountered the eƯects of an oil embargo 
before.  Most American’s weren’t alive when the so-called Arab Oil Embargo virtually crippled the 
American economy in the early ‘80s, giving rise to the term “stagﬂation” – runaway inﬂation 
combined with a resulting stagnation caused by interest-rate hikes applied by a hawkish Federal 
Reserve to bring inﬂation back down.  Muni bond yields reached 13-14% before yielding.  That’s 
almost 3x today’s level.

B.4  Loss of autonomous control over “calendar real estate”  
 
In the status quo, the City’s sovereignty over its own Broadway-capable building provides the 
latitude it needs for maximizing revenues and minimizing expenses.  By entering into a forced 
collaboration / competition with a nearby PSU student venue (perhaps even in the same building), 
the new venue relinquishing sole control.  There is no guarantee that the resulting operation will 
remain proﬁtable, PSU claims notwithstanding. 
 
There is no reason why a City-owned Broadway-capable Keller replacement on PSU’s site couldn’t 
be a solely-owned venue in its own separate building, just as it is today – except that conjoining it 
with PSU functions helps make the Project appear entirely education-related and hence eligible 
for state higher-ed bond ﬁnancing.   
 
As one PSU advocate wonders, “Wouldn’t it be great if students on their way to their rehearsal 
rooms were to bump into Renée Fleming in a hallway, and she held a master class right on the 
spot?” 
 
The PSU plan does achieve some economies of scale and elimination of redundancy – but they 
work generally to PSU’s advantage, not so much to the City’s – particularly the revenues ﬂowing 
from restaurant and bar operations in the concert-hall lobby.  (Note:  In the Vanport precedent, 
sublease revenue from street-level retail tenants goes solely to PSU -- AND their ﬂoor space counts 
toward PSU’s permanent 51% majority voting allocation.) 
 
Time is money, especially for ticket sales. It’s useful to view the Keller’s calendar as a scarce, hence 
valuable, ﬁnancial asset – call it “calendar real estate. ”  Viewing it this way is useful because it 
generates a perpetual stream of beneﬁts, cultural and economic, not only for arts organizations 
large and small, but for their diverse audiences, not to mention countless downtown businesses.

Ownership of its entire year’s calendar enables the current Keller to optimize its income by skillfully 
distributing 365 days of the year among Broadway shows (with their various travel schedules, load-
in / -out and equipment setup requirements) with the calendars and and other needs of the 
Portland Opera, the Oregon Ballet, Shen Yun, graduations and various other miscellaneous events 
large and small. 
 
Consider the traƯic, transit and parking congestion that would ensue if the two venues were to 
schedule major events on the same date. Consider the diƯiculty in sharing a lobby, bar and 
restaurant facilities, equipment and costume storage, restrooms, parking, marketing, crowd 
movement and signage.  Such collisions could simply not be allowed.  The entire calendar-ﬁlling job 
would become unimaginably more diƯicult if it all had to be done by permission granted by PSU, 
who would have a built-in conﬂict of interest favoring their own student venue. 
 
The only solutions I can see would be either to (1) set up some intermediating scheduling entity to 
arbitrate calendar conﬂicts, or (2) contractually partition the calendar into two timeshares. And 
either solution would amount to surrendering a crucial component of the City’s latitude in 
maximizing its own revenues. 
 
B.5  Withdrawal or failure of another project participant 
 
Given turbulence in the economy, it is not unlikely that either the hotel developer or some key 
contractor might fail to perform its duties, causing the whole house of cards to fall.  It would be 
irresponsible for the City to overlook this contingency, and the eƯect it would have on the City’s 
obligations as a partner.  Who would cover the missing partner’s share of project expenses?  In a 
condominium arrangement, it all depends on how the COA governance is structured. 
 
Customarily, a legitimate COA board would pass a “special assessment” to cover the unexpected 
expense.  If the COA board is wholly controlled by PSU, they alone would have the power to decide 
whose creditors get stuck with the bill.

template. ”

through a bond measure, everyone’s household budget. 
 
And that’s far from the worst-case scenario.  We’ve encountered the eƯects of an oil embargo

combined with a resulting stagnation caused by interest-rate hikes applied by a hawkish Federal 
Reserve to bring inﬂation back down.  Muni bond yields reached 13-14% before yielding.  That’s 
almost 3x today’s level.

The proposed remodeling of The Keller Auditorium would entail a two-year closure.

My earliest interest in this subject was the city’s urban renewal changes of the Civic Auditorium in the 
late ‘60s and continued following the city acquisition of the old Paramount Theater, its conversion into 
the Schnitzer Concert Hall and the subsequent construction of Antoinette Hatﬁeld Hall housing the 
Newmark, Winningstad and Brunish theaters. 
 
It is vital to understand that the city has a history of building things not by market need but in the hope 
that, once built, the market will materialize and make them ﬁnancially stable. I know from conversation 
with fellow artists and with former City Commissioner Mildred Schwab, that the City Council’s intention 
to build was with the assumption that availability would attract rental use regardless of the lack of 
demand for same. 
 
The Hatﬁeld Hall complex opened in 1987 and struggled ﬁnancially for two years before the city turned

put, the success of one would aid the losses of others, the tide raising all boats. This history, were it well-
known, ought to encourage voters to look more closely at city proposals to create new theaters where no 
market need is warranted.

In my years as a part-time theater artist, I have had the fortune of juggling careers in banking, commercial

PART III —THE NEWMARK THEATER 
ALLAN CLASSEN  
MAR 16, 2026 
 
 
The mid-sized Newmark has not found the economic sweet spot. 
 


--- PDF PAGE 145 ---
In 2025, Metro returned Portland’5 management to the city, effective 2027, without stressing that 
theater finances will no longer be supported by Metro’s broader budget. The city must now maximize 
rentals, retain earnings and fund capital upgrades. Its Performing Arts Venues Workgroup’s 
priorities—expand usage, strengthen local arts partnerships and improve booking policies—ultimately 
hinge on increasing rental revenue to ensure financial stability. 
The 900-seat Newmark Theater suits mid-sized productions with moderate break-even needs, such 
as local dance and theater companies, stand-up comics and small musical ensembles. However, no 
local theater company has sufficient subscription or ticket demand to fill 900 seats over multiple 
performances, nor the budget to cover union-scale move-in, move-out, stagehand, actor and 
musician costs. Most operate in 150–250 seat venues and lack the market demand to scale up. Only 
about half a dozen local companies can afford to rent this space even occasionally. 
In a 60-day sample, five local dance companies account for 22 of the booked days, though they do 
not sustain that volume year-round. The Metro Youth Symphony and Portland Opera add 10 days, 
and five additional events are single-night performances. Excluding possible move-in, move-out 
bookings, the Newmark remains dark 25 of the next 60 days. This rental sample is a close to a best-
case scenario and unlikely to repeat consistently, especially in summer. The conclusion is clear: The 
city’s Workgroup is correct—the Newmark needs substantially more bookings and sales. 
This raises a fundamental question of design and intent. Before the venue opened in 1987, what 
evidence demonstrated market demand for a 900-seat theater in Portland? Which well-capitalized 
local arts organizations with proven audiences could offer regular tenancy—and how was that need 
validated? 
History suggests the demand was overstated. The Oregon Shakespeare Festival satellite company 
couldn’t make a success of a regional transplant operations there—it imploded in crushing debt. 
Portland Civic Theater attempted to use the space, but its finances brought about ultimate bankruptcy 
and destruction. Oregon Children’s Theater Co. is another that could not financially survive 
attempting to provide entertainment there. Local independent producers who I know likewise could 
not recoup investment, let alone profit. The few who remain, survive solely on charity and choose 
their rental dates carefully in their season of events. 
Though I have not analyzed the full data, the issue appears to extend beyond one 900-seat theater, 
revealing a basic economic divide between local and national productions. A key factor is audience 
perception of quality: Many patrons assume that touring or national programs offer superior 
entertainment, while viewing local productions as inferior. They will pay $90–$120 for Broadway 
Across America, yet far fewer will spend $60 on a locally produced version of the same musical. 

--- PDF PAGE 146 ---
While touring casts are equity professionals using original or replicated New York sets and 
costumes—and quality differences likely exist—the performance gap may be narrower than assumed. 
Nonetheless, audiences will not pay comparable prices for local productions, even in the same 
venue. 
National producers can more easily absorb Newmark’s rental costs because they generate far greater 
gross revenue, but local companies cannot. All Portland’5 venues are union houses, requiring 
minimum union hires for stage crews, actors and related labor—costs equal for all producers. Most 
local companies do not face these union expenses in their own venues and cannot generate sufficient 
ticket revenue at necessary price points to cover them. This exposes a flawed city assumption: that 
local arts organizations could become substantial, regular lessees of this 900-seat space. They lack 
both the revenue scale and pricing power to sustain that level of production costs. 
Of 13 productions scheduled at the Newmark in the next 60 days, six are booked by national 
producers—two by Live Nation and one by AEG—accounting for six of the 33 booked days in this 
sample. None, local or national, function as anchor tenants providing consistent volume. Roughly half 
of bookings come from out-of-town producers. Currently, this is the only venue of this size. If a new 
city-operated Portland State University theater entered the market, it would not double demand but 
divide it -- splitting bookings between two properties and leaving both underutilized, where previously 
one might remain profitable. 
In summary, the Newmark operates at roughly 40–45% vacancy, underperforming in both bookings 
and revenue. Its scale and union requirement effectively exclude most local arts companies from 
renting regularly or at a break-even level. Those that do rent depend on substantial charitable 
support, often underwriting losses, and none provide consistent, profitable lease income. 
At 900 seats, the venue serves a limited market and makes profitability hard to achieve. Nearly half of 
recent lessees are national producers presenting single-night events—the least lucrative model—
requiring higher volume to compensate for limited dates. Two of these producers, Live Nation and 
AEG, present additional long-term concerns for Portland’5 that remain largely unaddressed. 
Taken together, current patterns suggest an increasing need for stronger management and more 
aggressive sales strategy at Portland’5. And there is more yet to examine.

If you build it ... they still won't come 
Part IV – Portland'5's Smallest Theaters Struggling 
 
Northwest Examiner 
by Kurt Misar 
Mar 19, 2026 
 
 
Even with frequent Stumptown Stages bookings, the Brunish Theater is empty more often than not. 
With the city of Portland assuming management of Portland’5 in 2027, theater ﬁnances will no longer be 
supported by Metro’s broader budget. Making them self-supporting will not be easy. 
Winningstad Theater 
Each theater in Hatﬁeld Hall and its three theaters, serves a distinct niche. The Newmark is a traditional 
proscenium with little ﬂexibility between stage, pit, and seating. The Winningstad, by contrast, was 
designed for adaptable use, including thrust staging with audience on three sides, though that ﬂexibility 
is seldom used. It seats about 300—close to the 150–250 range most local companies prefer for 
economic viability. (The Armory Mainstage seats up to 560 but does not ﬁll it consistently and Artist 
Repertory Theatre’s proposed new mainstage would seat 178.) 


--- PDF PAGE 149 ---
The Winningstad, ﬂexible or not, primarily serves local companies without permanent homes and small 
touring acts that don’t require large revenue to break even. Local groups need this smaller venue due to 
their modest ticket sales and pricing. 
In a 60-day sample, two single-performer events booked one day each, a traveling magician duo books 
two days, and Stumptown Stages ran a 23-day musical—for a total of 27 booked days out of 60, leaving 
more than half the dates unﬁlled. Only four rental days at the Winningstad were booked by national 
producers 
Stumptown does not publish prior venue data but advertises Hatﬁeld Hall as its home, implying anchor 
status. Assuming ﬁve shows running 25 days each in 2025–26, it booked 125 dates across the three 
stages (including Winningstad and Brunish). Without Stumptown’s current 60-day Winningstad run, the 
theater would be dark 56 of 60 days. Without Stumptown, the stage would be eƯectively bankrupt. 
 
 
The ﬂex-space Winningstad Theatre rarely gets national bookings. 
Brunish Theater 
This theater is essentially a large box with seating akin to movable bleachers for about 145 seats, suitable 
only for ﬂexible but limited-production events. It is the least expensive of the ﬁve to rent and generates 
the lowest gross sales by seat count. 
In a 60-day sample, Stumptown Stages occupies roughly 30 days, and Portland Shakespeare Project 
rents one day. Both present small-cast, simple-set productions to control costs. Like the Winningstad, 
the Brunish is operating annually at under 50% capacity and is signiﬁcantly under-rented. 


--- PDF PAGE 150 ---
Stumptown’s rentals are split between two, possibly three, stages. Typically, Stumptown books 50–60 
days per year in either the Newmark or Winningstad and 75–90 days in the Brunish. For the Winningstad 
and Brunish, their largest tenant occupies about 60 of 360 days in one theater and 90 in the other—by any 
measure, not enough to anchor their ﬁnancial viability. 
Currently, the ﬁnancial stability of the aging Winningstad and Brunish theaters rests largely on local arts 
companies operating under substantial ﬁnancial risk. At more than 50% annual vacancy rate, these 
stages cannot generate suƯicient income for operations, maintenance, repairs and capital upgrades 
without city subsidy. 
How long can this continue? The city’s Performing Arts Venue Workgroup clearly recognized this dilemma 
in its stated management goals. 
And wait ‘til you hear the rest of the story.

If you build it ... they still won't come 
 
Part V: Two private theaters could change everything 
 
by Kurt Misar 
NW Examiner 
Mar 21, 2026 
 
 
Will Live Nation continue to rent from the city when it has its own Portland auditorium? 
 
In 2025, METRO returned Portland’5 management to the city eƯective 2027—without emphasizing that 
theater ﬁnances will no longer be subsidized by Metro’s broader budget. The city must now maximize 
rentals, retain earnings, and fund capital upgrades. The city’s Workgroup stated goals—expanded usage, 
stronger arts partnerships, improved booking policies—ultimately depend on one thing: increasing rental 
revenue. 
 
In this series, I withheld an important point so readers could ﬁrst understand the operating realities of 
these ﬁve city-owned venues. I reviewed rental calendars over several months to assess vacancy, usage, 
and lease activity to determine whether each venue was maximizing income potential. I had no access to 
proﬁt-and-loss statements, so this analysis addresses market demand—not accounting performance. 
 


--- PDF PAGE 152 ---
The question was simple: Is there suƯicient producer demand to justify the city owning and operating ﬁve 
stages—let alone building a sixth? 
 
The Keller (roughly 3,000 seats) appears to be the most utilized at 80-85% of rental capacity, almost 
entirely dependent on a single anchor tenant. With a minimum two-year retroﬁt closure looming, the city 
stands to lose that anchor to Portland State University, which is proposing its own 3,000-seat theater on 
its campus. 
 
That proposal sidesteps an obvious option: transferring the anchor to the roughly 3,000-seat Schnitzer, 
which currently operates near 75% capacity. Of Schnitzer’s remaining rentals, approximately 25% come 
from Live Nation and AEG, with another 25% from outside producers. 
 
As for the three other Portland’5 stages next door in Hatﬁeld Hall, the Newmark, currently near 50% 
capacity, relies on many of these same outside producers, along with short-term local rentals. The 
Winningstad and Brunish operate well under 50% capacity and remain heavily dependent on a single 
local anchor. These ﬁgures suggest insuƯicient market demand to support additional theater space. The 
objective appears less about expanding arts access and more about replacing The Keller during its 
closure. 
 
I do not know why the Schnitzer cannot serve as a temporary home for Broadway Across America. The 
producers may object to logistics or technical limitations, but it is not the only alternative 3,000-seat 
venue in Portland—because two more are about to open. 
 
Now for the withheld point. It matters because it reframes how the city presents its development strategy. 
 
Live Nation and AEG 
 
Live Nation is constructing a privately owned 1,250-3,500 seat venue on Southeast Water Avenue. AEG, 
partnering with Monqui Presents, is building a 2,000–4,250-seat venue at Lloyd Center. Both open before 
any potential Keller closure. Both will require aggressive booking to recoup construction costs. The city 
already struggles to ﬁll its existing venues. Soon it will compete with two new private operators seeking 
many of the same touring acts. 
 
Before speculating about long-term market fragmentation, consider the immediate impact. As producers 
developing touring acts, Live Nation and AEG are likely to shift many of their current Schnitzer and 
Newmark bookings into their own facilities. That accounts for roughly 25% of Schnitzer’s current rental 
activity—potentially pushing annual vacancy there toward 50%—with similar eƯects at the Newmark. At 
that level, sustained proﬁtability becomes doubtful. 
 
Ironically, those losses could free space at the Schnitzer to absorb Broadway Across America dates 
displaced from the Keller. 
 

--- PDF PAGE 153 ---
Still, market behavior is never perfectly predictable. Private operators will ﬁrst prioritize their own 
productions—primarily music and comedy—but they will also seek bookings from the same national and 
regional independent producers who historically rent the Schnitzer, Newmark and Winningstad. The city 
does not want to lose the Broadway Across America business, but is that likely? 
 
Traditionally, BAA occupies municipal theaters designed for touring musicals. It is unclear whether they 
envision a future in privately owned venues primarily oriented toward pop, rock, country and national 
comics—but ﬁnancial incentives can change preferences quickly. A suƯiciently favorable deal from a 
new operator could undercut city pricing and shift the calculus overnight. And each will oƯer seating 
capacities in excess of The Keller Auditorium. 
 
BAA maintains strong subscription sales and deep market presence in Portland. If the stage product 
remains identical, would a venue shift aƯect ticket demand? And if so, how would relocation to a PSU-
based facility diƯer? 
 
In the next article, we will examine this, including how government seems to distort and misrepresent 
market realities to achieve broader objectives.

If you build it… they still won’t come 
 
Part VI – Room for 2? How about 5? 
 
By Kurt Misar 
March 25, 2025  
 
 
 
 
The need to update Keller Auditorium led to a cascade of questionable propositions. 
 
Before moving forward with the last two installments in this series, a correction is in order, based on 
the recent city-commissioned market feasibility study. 
 
Our earlier sampling of Keller Auditorium usage was too optimistic. Hunden Partners reported the 
Keller has an average annual utilization rate of 58%. Our previous 120-day sampling suggested 80-
85% utilization, allowing for move-in and move-out days. And that period didn’t include the slower 
summer season. 
 
Hunden was asked to assess whether Portland could sustain both a remodeled Keller and a new 
Portland State University auditorium and concluded that it could not. Assuming that is true, how will 
the market absorb two additional privately funded 3,000-seat houses now under construction? 
 
The study acknowledges that an additional PSU venue would further fracture the market and reduce 
profitability for all operators. It also concedes, but ignores, that the coming privately funded 
auditoriums by AEG and Live Nation will complicate the competitive landscape. Nevertheless, it 
recommends expansion in the form of a new 3,000-seat venue on the PSU campus. 
 
Closing the Keller two years for renovation could interrupt Broadway Across America performances 
there, but city-owned Schnitzer Auditorium could accommodate BAA, at least as a temporary 
measure. By this summer, the 3,000-plus-seat Live Nation venue should be in operation, followed by 
AEG’s similarly sized facility in 2027. 
 


--- PDF PAGE 155 ---
The study claims local arts organizations would face hardship without a large-scale venue. Yet, if the 
Keller were temporarily shuttered for renovation, Portland would still have two large houses available 
within six months, and three within 12—long before PSU could possibly break ground. If Keller were 
retained post-retrofit, that number rises to four. With PSU, the total could reach five. This not only 
contradicts the report’s conclusion that the market cannot sustain two venues—also shown in this 
series—but endorses a path that could produce four or five 3,000 seat venues that defy market logic. 
 
While the booking sample of the Schnitzer suggests that it operates in the black, that is before the 
Live Nation and AEG bookings disappear into their own facilities. Once they stop booking, the 
Schnitzer will likely fall to 50% vacancy, making it more available for BAA events during Keller 
renovation. 
 
Meanwhile, the Hatfield Hall trio of stages appear to operate below 50% occupancy. The Keller is 
barely 60% utilized, and the rest of the city’s mid-sized inventory is significantly under-performing. 
Meanwhile, Portland Opera Association has paused its next season, needing to raise $500,000 in 
charitable donations to resume. The POA has been retooling its seasons for at least a decade. 
Somehow, the public framing of the debate emphasizes protecting venue availability rather than 
confronting whether some resident companies can afford large houses at all. 
 
This debate ultimately comes down to arithmetic rather than aspiration. When occupancy averages 
45-58%, when mid-sized venues operate below half capacity, and when new competitors are entering 
the same finite marketplace, the rational question is not how to build more, but how to manage what 
already exists more responsibly. Before taxpayers commit hundreds of millions of dollars to another 
3,000-seat hall, the city must come to grips with realities. The issue is not scarcity. It is stewardship. 
 
The last installment examines what may be the most persistent fiction of all: the myth of scarcity.

If you build it… they still won’t come 
 
Part VII - The Myth of Scarcity 
 
by Kurt Misar 
March 31, 2025 
  
 
 
 
Hunden Partners has advised the city that two public performing arts auditoriums in Portland are not economically 
feasible and therefore the Keller Auditorium should be demolished and a new one built by Portland State University. 
 
The problem with the Hunden report is not merely its conclusion, but the framing used to reach it. The 
document repeatedly presents possibility as probability, amplifies speculative risks and minimizes 
contradictions within its own market assumptions. Its language reveals a pattern: 
 
 Worst-case scenarios are emphasized, competitive realities are deferred, and renovation is 
quietly dismissed in favor of replacement. 


--- PDF PAGE 157 ---
 
 It becomes difficult to wade through the joint city and PSU press release rhetoric without 
encountering statements that overreach or misdirect: 
 
“Its (Keller Auditorium) closure could prevent performances from Broadway Across 
America for years and cause local arts organizations to face significant challenges 
without a large-scale performing venue. Portland State University’s proposed facility 
would be headlined by a 3,000-seat venue, capable of large-scale performances, but 
could hinder the long-term feasibility of the Portland’5 venues, particularly as two new 
live entertainment facilities from AEG and Live Nation are to be opened in the coming 
years.” 
 
These claims deserve careful parsing. 
 
First, the suggestion that closure “could prevent performances… for years” implies permanence. A 
retrofit interruption might last two years. It also assumes that BAA would not relocate temporarily to 
the Schnitzer or to one of the two new private 3,000-plus-seat venues opening within the next year. 
An interruption of venue does not equal its disappearance. 
 
Second, the study warns of “significant challenges without a large-scale performing venue.” Yet by 
summer 2026, there will be at least two 3,000-seat venues, and by early 2027, that reality contradicts 
the report’s assertion that the region cannot financially sustain two such facilities. With a Keller 
retrofit, Portland would have four. If PSU were added, five. The contradiction is self-evident. 
 
Third, the study concedes that a new PSU theater “could hinder the long-term feasibility of the 
Portland’5 venues.” On that point, the authors are refreshingly candid. Additional stage supply 
fragments the already finite demand. The report recognizes this, ignores it, dismisses it and yet still 
recommends building another venue. 
 
Fourth, the competitive impact of AEG and Live Nation is framed as something occurring “in the 
coming years,” when in fact those facilities will be operating well before any Keller retrofit or PSU 
project breaks ground. The market effects will be immediate, not hypothetical. 
 
The study’s most pointed claims appear in these summary bullet arguments: 
 
 “(Keller is a) …very wide auditorium that creates many limited view seats, and it features 
outdated amenities, lowering customer experience.” (“Many” is asserted without quantification, 
and no evidence is offered to demonstrate sales deterrence. Limited sightlines and dated 
amenities are conditions that can be addressed in renovation. They are not proof of terminal 
decline.) 
 
 “A multi-year closure of Keller risks eroding Portland’s Broadway Across America audience and 
forcing skilled labor to relocate from Portland to other cities.” (Risk does not equal inevitability. 
Audience erosion presumes no temporary relocation strategy and no substitution venue. 

--- PDF PAGE 158 ---
Skilled labor displacement is assumed despite two major private venues under construction 
and announcing substantial hiring.) 
 
 “Private competition from new commercial venues could also siphon concerts and events from 
the Keller, which creates an additional commercial demand reduction if there were to be two 
large theater-style venues.” (By early 2027, there will be at least three such venues regardless 
of Keller’s fate.) 
 
 “New venues in other markets have resulted in sustained audience and subscription growth, 
higher customer satisfaction and improved production capability.” (Even if true elsewhere, that 
does not justify demolition here. Nor does it establish that modernization is equivalent to a new 
structure.) 
 
Notice how frequently “possibility” is presented as “certain probability,” and how often conjecture 
substitutes for evidence. 
 
Meanwhile, the study asserts that the greater Portland market cannot support two large venues like 
the Keller. Yet the city proposes constructing another 3,000-seat facility rather than renovating the 
one it owns. In a finite market, adding supply further fragments revenue streams. 
 
For example, Portland Opera Association has recently paused moving forward with a new season, 
seeking $500,000 in underwriting before proceeding. That is not a venue problem. It is a demand and 
cost-balance problem. The city is aware of these financial stresses, yet frames the discussion 
primarily around preserving access to the most expensive house in town. 
 
We do not need more theaters. We need to ensure the long-term value of the ones we already 
possess. Whether government—or my own industry—has the skills and discipline to do that remains 
uncertain. 
 
The full study is available on the city’s website. Make your own assessments and comment below so 
we can address them.
