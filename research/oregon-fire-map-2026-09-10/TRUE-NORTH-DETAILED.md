# Fire in Oregon: the project’s true north

## 1. The purpose and the flagship deliverable

**Help people understand how fire works, what we can change, and what it actually takes—in money, people, time, and repeated work—to make an Oregon landscape more resilient.**

The most productive part of your interview was the progression from “Why aren’t we doing more?” to concrete questions: Who would do the work? What would it cost? What happens afterward? What evidence would tell us the investment helped?

Those questions should organize both the website and the research.

Build two connected products:

| Product | Audience | What it delivers |
|---|---|---|
| **The public guide** | Curious Oregonians, students, educators | One coherent visual story explaining fire, management choices, costs, outcomes, and uncertainty. |
| **One landscape investigation** | The public, practitioners, funders, policymakers | A documented account of what has been done, what remains, what delivery requires, and what different levels of investment could accomplish. |

The landscape investigation supplies real examples and numbers for the guide. The guide makes the investigation understandable beyond specialists.

Our contribution should complement existing work. ODF is developing both an internal accomplishment-and-finance tracker and an interagency tracking system. We should seek compatible identifiers and exports while concentrating on the explanation connecting investment, implementation, maintenance, and outcomes. [ODF implementation report, page 6](https://www.oregon.gov/odf/aboutodf/documents/2025-odf-sb83-landscape-resilency-strategy-implementation-report.pdf)

**The first flagship deliverable:** a public, reproducible landscape report answering:

> What would it take to carry out and maintain the documented priorities here, what would that work protect or improve, and how would we know whether it helped?

It includes an illustrated website chapter, a detailed landscape page, downloadable evidence and calculations, and a short decision brief.

**Project decision rule:** add a dataset, feature, interview, or visualization when it helps answer a named reader question or resolve a specific uncertainty in the pilot. Keep other discoveries in the backlog.

The initial operating model is **Edan leading an AI-assisted research and publishing workflow**. It does not assume a paid research team, committed institutional partners, or unrestricted access to government records.

## 2. Turn the interview’s questions into the public story

Keep `/oregon-fire` as the continuous introduction, with roughly 12–15 minutes of core reading. Reuse the strongest existing components and deeper pages. Organize the material around this sequence:

| Chapter | Reader’s question | Main visual |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Fire changes a place** | Why can one fire help a landscape while another causes lasting harm? | An annotated landscape showing different effects within one documented fire. |
| **2. Places have different histories** | Why isn’t there one answer for all of Oregon? | Oregon landscape comparison connecting vegetation, climate, stewardship, and fire history. |
| **3. We have several tools** | Why burn, thin, protect homes, or sometimes allow wildfire to continue? | A comparison showing what each intervention changes and what it cannot accomplish. |
| **4. Location matters** | How do we decide where work belongs? | The pilot landscape with documented community, water, habitat, and treatment priorities. |
| **5. A plan must become work** | Why doesn’t an approved project immediately happen? | A real project timeline showing preparation, approvals, contracting, crews, weather windows, completion, and delays. |
| **6. What does the money buy?** | What does prevention cost, who pays, and how often must work repeat? | A spending breakdown paired with a maintenance timeline. |
| **7. Did it help?** | How can we measure benefits when the alternative didn’t happen? | Observations alongside a clearly explained research comparison and its uncertainty. |
| **8. What can change next?** | What can residents, communities, agencies, and funders actually do? | Specific decisions and participation opportunities tied to documented needs. |

### Use your questions directly

Develop short, independently sourced answers to:

- If we spend so much fighting fires, why not spend more beforehand?
- What would another million dollars actually buy in this place?
- Is money the constraint, or are crews, preparation, and timing the constraint?
- Why treat one area before another?
- Does thinning finish the job?
- How long does treatment last, and who pays for maintenance?
- Why can’t selling the wood or producing energy pay for everything?
- Why don’t we simply let wildfires burn?
- How do researchers estimate damage that was avoided?
- How do we compare landscape work, home protection, utility work, and firefighting?

Place each answer where it advances the story. Provide a question index linking to those sections and deeper explanations; avoid appending a disconnected FAQ.

**Keep the interview private.** You explicitly promised Jenna that her words would not be used without permission. Use the conversation to identify research questions, not as publishable testimony. Public answers must stand on public evidence. Any attributed interview requires separate permission. Retain Edan and Dominic as public authors.

- Keep desktop body text around 18–20 pixels, constrain prose width, and reserve oversized typography for the opening.
- Give photographs dates, locations, credits, and useful annotations. Use diagrams for mechanisms that photographs cannot explain.
- Integrate existing videos beside the question they answer, with a short takeaway and transcript link. Reading the page must not require watching them.
- Put detailed methods and qualifications beside the relevant number or in expandable notes. Avoid repeated defensive disclaimers.
- Preserve mobile readability, keyboard access, reduced-motion behavior, static alternatives, and existing shared links.

Move the current hypothetical calculator out of the main narrative. Replace its prominence with an actual documented cost example. A deeper teaching exercise may remain, but its assumptions must be explicit and its outputs must not resemble Oregon forecasts.

## 3. Investigate one landscape, with a defensible method

### Select the pilot through evidence and access

Compare these three candidates during the first two weeks:

| Candidate | Starting material | Critical access question |
|---|---|---|
| **Ashland/Rogue Valley** | AFR monitoring, project history, community planning, existing southern Oregon contacts. [Monitoring](https://ashlandoregon.gov/201/Science-Monitoring), [2025 CWPP](https://www.ashlandoregon.gov/DocumentCenter/View/4053/250624_Ashland_CWPP_FINAL) | Can partners connect expenditures and staffing to identifiable treatment sequences and maintenance? |
| **Central Oregon/Deschutes** | Collaborative restoration reports, treatment records, existing practitioner relationships. [CFLRP report](https://www.fs.usda.gov/restoration/documents/cflrp/2023AnnualReports/FY2023%20Deschutes%20CFLRP%20Annual%20Report_Final%20Feb%2028%202024.pdf) | Can we obtain a bounded project’s financial records, implementation history, and capacity constraints? |
| **Wallowa/northeastern Oregon** | Community stewardship programs, contractor relationships, restoration reporting. [Wallowa Resources](https://www.wallowaresources.org/) | Can restoration reporting be separated into the specific activities and costs relevant to fire resilience? |

Score candidates on financial documentation, 30%; identifiable activities and geography, 25%; responsive custodians, 20%; monitoring, 15%; and community relevance, 10%.

Require an identifiable project boundary, at least one completed activity with attributable expenditure records, and a custodian willing to clarify records. Select the highest-scoring eligible candidate. If none qualifies, publish the initial guide improvements and continue acquisition without presenting an incomplete pilot as a comprehensive account.

Use the selected program’s actual boundary. Investigate the landscape’s overall plan, then reconstruct three to five treatment sequences in detail. Include maintenance or a delayed/cancelled activity when records support it.

### Assemble the evidence in five connected parts

**A. The place and the decisions**

Obtain the adopted plan, objectives, alternatives, decision documents, unit boundaries, public priorities, and amendments. Establish who decided what and why. Distinguish the project boundary, treatment units, wildfire perimeters, and measured effects.

**B. The work and delivery constraints**

Connect planning, preparation, thinning, disposal, burning, monitoring, and maintenance through identifiers and dates. Request existing work plans, contracts, accomplishments, staffing summaries, and documented postponements.

Measure crew-days, specialist availability, procurement time, and other constraints only where records support them. Do not infer a staffing shortage from a delay alone.

**C. The money**

Trace funding through appropriation or award, implementing organization, contract, expenditure, and activity where possible.

Keep grants and transfers separate from final expenditure; the same dollar may appear in several organizations’ records. Separate planning, implementation, monitoring, maintenance, receipts, and overhead.

Every amount needs its period, geography, accounting basis, source, and estimate/final status. Missing costs stay unknown.

Start the statewide context with bounded published accounts. For example, ODF reports approximately **$50.98 million expended through thirteen programs in calendar 2025**. That is a defined reporting scope, not Oregon’s total fire spending or a pure prescribed-fire budget. [ODF implementation report, pages 1–2](https://www.oregon.gov/odf/aboutodf/documents/2025-odf-sb83-landscape-resilency-strategy-implementation-report.pdf)

**D. Maintenance**

Document what must recur, what triggers it, who is responsible, and whether funding exists. Separate an initial treatment sequence from later maintenance. Do not impose a universal reburn interval or percentage cost reduction.

**E. Results**

Separate completed work, measured ecological or community outcomes, modeled effects, and manager assessments. Track both benefits and burdens, including smoke, access disruptions, implementation risks, and who pays.

Acres treated remain an activity measure. They do not become a universal measure of resilience.

### Build the economics explanation in stages

First explain actual spending. Then explain published benefit estimates. Only then introduce local scenarios supported by local inputs.

One relevant study estimates **$5–6 in suppression-cost savings per treatment dollar** for its Pacific Northwest Forest Service setting. A separate study estimates **$3.73 in expected avoided-damage benefits per dollar** across its western study population. These concern different outcomes and methods; do not add them or apply either multiplier automatically to the pilot. [Economic Journal study](https://doi.org/10.1093/ej/ueag037), [authors’ report](https://www.perc.org/2026/05/07/beyond-wildfire-suppression/)

Inspect the available [replication code and data documentation](https://github.com/frederikstrabo/cost-effective-fuel-treatments). Reproduce a bounded published result before adapting anything. In particular, distinguish imputed treatment costs from transaction-level expenditures.

The eventual local comparison has three cases:

1. Currently funded work.
2. The documented priority program, including maintenance.
3. A feasible expansion reviewed by the local custodian.

Show work, timing, staffing requirements, costs, and unresolved assumptions. Publish monetary avoided-loss estimates only if the method supports local application and receives qualified review. Otherwise explain the benefits supported by evidence without inventing a local savings total.

Do not make a statewide “dollars saved per acre” map an initial deliverable.

## 4. Run the project through a written, AI-assisted evidence workflow

### Establish one durable project record

Create a canonical charter in the existing research directory and link it prominently from its README. Preserve previous plans as dated history.

Maintain four connected registers:

- **Questions and claims:** reader question, proposed answer, sources, page/table locators, limitations, reviewer, and publication status.
- **Acquisition:** missing record, responsible custodian, request scope, thread reference, response, and the decision it would enable.
- **Pilot evidence:** projects, activities, funding records, capacity observations, monitoring, and verified relationships.
- **Delivery backlog:** public improvement, dependency, acceptance test, priority, and current status.

Store transcripts, email bodies, recipient lists, and restricted attachments privately outside the repository and deployment inputs. Public manifests contain only approved material.

### Divide responsibilities clearly

**AI handles:** finding public sources and contacts, checking existing correspondence, drafting requests, preserving attachments, extracting tables, identifying contradictions, performing reproducible calculations, preparing graphics and site changes, and running verification.

**Edan handles:** relationships, scope decisions, meetings when necessary, disputed interpretations, and approval of substantive new claims and releases.

Seek small, written reviews from practitioners, researchers, and educators. Reviewers advise on specific material; participation does not imply institutional endorsement. Do not assume Jenna or Dominic has committed to ongoing review work.

For each incoming reply:

1. Preserve the original and attachments privately.
2. Extract the facts with exact document locators.
3. Compare them with existing claims and records.
4. Identify what is resolved and what remains uncertain.
5. Prepare the smallest necessary clarification.
6. Present the proposed public change and evidence for review.
7. Publish approved changes with a dated correction or update when appropriate.

Silence from a source does not validate our interpretation.

### Prioritize outreach by the question it resolves

| Contact group | Initial request |
|---|---|
| ODF strategy/data custodians | Existing financial/accomplishment exports, identifiers, coverage, and how to complement LRAFT and interagency work. |
| Three candidate landscape programs | One representative completed-project packet and identification of its financial and operational custodians. |
| OSU Extension | Review the highest-priority public questions and introduce an appropriate local specialist or educator. |
| Oregon Prescribed Fire Council and Sustainable Northwest | Introductions to practitioners with documented delivery and maintenance examples. |
| Economic-study authors | Clarify cost construction, uncertainty, replication access, and whether findings transfer to a local pilot. |
| Selected landscape’s community, tribal, water, and smoke/public-health contacts | Identify priorities, burdens, omitted perspectives, and appropriate use of their information. |

Inspect public records and existing email threads first. Request one or two useful existing documents rather than a custom report or the entire twelve-item packet at once. Prefer email; offer a brief meeting only when it would resolve something specific.

Use verified public addresses, the existing sending account, duplicate protection, and the shared email gate. Keep initial batches small and sends sequential. Sending and follow-ups must remain within the user’s authorized scope; an ongoing project plan does not create an unattended email campaign.

### Correct the notes before using them as evidence

Maintain a private claim audit. Specifically:

- The “$4 per acre” first-entry figure is internally inconsistent; do not silently change it to $4,000.
- The staffing estimate generated by AI is not evidence.
- Federal budget percentages, funding expiration, and organizational changes require dated primary sources.
- Reforestation belongs under recovery/restoration when that is its purpose.
- Suppression firing and prescribed burning require distinct explanations.
- Claims about litigation, treatment scale, biomass economics, or avoided disaster need their own evidence.
- Small projects may have habitat, cultural, training, or local protection value even when they cannot change an entire wildfire’s behavior.

## 5. Implementation, release sequence, and acceptance

### Reuse the existing application

Keep the current map, ingestion framework, project dossiers, evidence validation, and shareable URLs.

Extend the reviewed data structures with landscape membership, funding relationships, capacity observations, scenarios, and question-to-evidence links. Preserve original source IDs and distinguish confirmed relationships from candidates.

The first editorial release needs no new public API. The pilot adds:

- `/oregon-fire/landscapes/[slug]` for the illustrated investigation.
- `GET /api/oregon-fire/landscapes/[slug]` for published evidence and scenario assumptions.
- A versioned downloadable table package with a data dictionary and provenance.

Private correspondence must never be accessible through those interfaces. Calculations run during analysis and publication, not inside reader requests.

### First 90 days

| Stage | Deliverable | Completion condition |
|---|---|---|
| **Weeks 1–2** | Charter, private interview audit, public-question register, candidate comparison, targeted requests | Every priority request has a clear purpose; pilot selection follows the evidence/access gate. |
| **Weeks 3–4** | First guide revision, improved costs explanation, one-page project brief | Readers can follow the main argument without opening the map or calculator. |
| **Weeks 5–8** | Pilot work history, financial account, maintenance evidence, initial visuals | At least one expenditure-to-completed-work chain reconciles; gaps and unmatched records remain visible. |
| **Weeks 9–12** | Reviewed landscape report, supported scenarios, downloadable evidence, educator trial | Claims and calculations pass review; no unsupported local savings estimates are published. |

This is a working sequence, not a promise about agency response times. Release verified explanations while records remain pending. If modeling evidence is insufficient, release the documented cost and delivery account and specify the research needed next.

Prepare a funding brief from the pilot’s demonstrated gaps: specialist economic review, missing data acquisition, community participation, and design or classroom testing. Do not make fundraising a prerequisite for useful early releases.

### Acceptance tests

**Understanding:** Test with at least five readers unfamiliar with fire management, including an educator or student where practical. At least four should correctly explain why treatment location matters, why work repeats, why a permit is not a completed burn, and why a study’s savings estimate is not a guarantee.

**Evidence:** Every published number has a source, scope, period, unit, and method. Observations, estimates, scenarios, and interpretations are distinguishable. No grant-transfer double counting, cumulative incident-cost summation, invented dates, or missing values converted to zero.

**Calculations:** Reproduce results from versioned inputs. Test repeated treatments, mixed fiscal/calendar years, partial completion, overlapping funding, and changed assumptions. Preserve a prior successful release.

**Experience:** Verify desktop/mobile layout, keyboard access, readable charts, static fallbacks, existing shared URLs, source links, exports, and correction forms. Run relevant typechecks, tests, lint, build, and focused browser checks.

**Privacy:** Confirm transcripts, identities withheld from publication, private correspondence, and restricted attachments are absent from HTML, APIs, downloads, search metadata, and social images.

**Practical usefulness:** A reader can identify what was done, what remains, who must act, what it costs, what evidence supports the benefits, and what is still unknown. A practitioner can trace those statements back to records and correct a specific item.

That becomes the continuing standard for the project: **every release should make a real fire-management choice easier to understand and better supported by evidence.**
