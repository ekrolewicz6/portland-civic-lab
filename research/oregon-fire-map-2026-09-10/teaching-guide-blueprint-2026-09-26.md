# Fire in Oregon: a coherent visual teaching guide

September 26, 2026. Editorial and product proposal following a review of the live main page and its component composition. This document changes no public application behavior. Public authors remain Edan Krolewicz and Dominic Kuklawood. Earlier acquisition, privacy, reconciliation and deployment requirements still apply.

## 1. The problem to solve

The existing page is a useful atlas, but it does not establish a sequence of understanding. A novice encounters landscape tabs, place search and a large map before learning how fire behaves or how to interpret its effects. Forest acreage, three case studies, imagery, burn windows, outcomes and source coverage follow as separate sections. The reader must supply the connections.

Important explanations are hidden in optional tabs. Examples vary in evidentiary strength without a stated selection method: Woodpecker documents intentions and implementation; Egley is an effectiveness study; Finley is a program description. These are useful for different questions, but they do not collectively establish statewide effectiveness. Repeated warnings about evidence replace opportunities to teach what evidence actually shows. The source table primarily describes ingestion, whereas the reader needs ecological, community and financial interpretation.

The redesign must make a coherent explanation the primary experience. The atlas remains available throughout and as a dedicated reference experience. Adding more independent cards will not solve the underlying problem.

## 2. The central question and intended learning

**Why do we fight some fires and deliberately light others—and how do we decide what to do in a particular place?**

After the core guide, a reader should be able to:

1. Explain how fuel, moisture, wind and terrain influence fire, including surface fire, canopy fire and embers.
2. Distinguish the energy released during a fire from the ecological changes afterward.
3. Explain why dry forest, wet forest, oak/prairie and sagebrush landscapes require different interpretations.
4. Connect Indigenous stewardship, interrupted stewardship, land use, fire exclusion, climate and community exposure without reducing every landscape's history to one cause.
5. Describe what prescribed fire, mechanical preparation, home/community protection and incident response each address.
6. Read a perimeter, severity assessment and later vegetation observation without treating them as interchangeable.
7. Distinguish money spent, projected expenditures, possible avoided losses and nonfinancial outcomes.
8. Identify who can act locally, what the relevant public plan says, and what evidence would establish progress.

Teaching the process of choosing and checking a strategy is the goal. A universal acreage quota or a single preferred intervention is not the lesson.

## 3. First screen and first minute

Proposed heading: **Fire in Oregon**.

Proposed opening question: **Why do we fight some fires and deliberately light others?**

Draft introductory copy, requiring normal scientific review before publication:

> Fire can kill trees, leave large trees alive, or help keep a prairie open. The result depends on the place, the conditions and what burns. Follow the fire from the ground to the treetops, see what people can change, and examine what those choices cost.

The opening visual should establish a question through three documented scenes: surviving large trees after a surface fire, a canopy-changing wildfire patch, and a managed prairie. Each has a location and date. Use the scenes to ask “What changed here?” rather than using an attractive fire photograph as decoration. Acquire and clear actual assets; original explanatory drawings can support the mechanism while photographs are being acquired.

One main action: **Start the guide**. Secondary persistent links: **Explore the atlas** and **For teachers**. Keep current local search available, but a place parameter should offer local evidence alongside the lesson rather than silently skipping the foundations.

Target a 12–15-minute core reading experience, with a longer reference route. This is a design target to measure in testing, not a current reading-time claim.

## 4. Eight connected chapters and their visuals

Each chapter contains a reader question, a short answer, one dominant visual, a documented example and a transition explaining why the next question follows. Optional detail contains methods, citations and additional evidence. Essential conclusions must remain visible without operating a control.

| Chapter | Reader's question | Main visual and interaction | Evidence or acquisition requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. What happens in a fire? | How can fire burn through a forest while leaving some trees alive? | An annotated forest cross-section showing surface fuels, small trees, canopy and ember paths. Simple stepped comparisons vary one factor at a time. | OSU fire-behavior and ecology publications; review of the illustration's mechanism. Qualitative diagrams must not report invented spread rates or flame lengths. |
| 2. Why does place matter? | Why might another fire help one landscape and damage another? | An Oregon ecological map linked to side-by-side landscape cross-sections and measured examples. Explain dry pine, southwest mixed conifer, western forests, oak/prairie and sagebrush; account for high-elevation environments in detailed references. | Reviewed regional ecology and vegetation geography. Boundaries must describe the actual mapped classification, not classify the surroundings of a town from its reference point. |
| 3. What changed? | Why are today's decisions different from those of the past? | A dated, located history combining repeat photographs, tree-ring evidence, land-use and stewardship history, weather/climate and settlement exposure. Separate panels make the geography of each piece explicit. | Original images and captions, local fire histories, public tribal-authored resources and appropriate collaboration, regional climate research. Photos show change; attribution requires additional evidence. |
| 4. What can people change? | What are the options, and why is a burn sometimes postponed? | A treatment sequence: objective → preparation → weather/smoke/staff decision → ignition → maintenance. A second diagram follows embers into a community and shows different protective actions. | Project decisions and plans, OSU planning guidance, NIST ignition research, local community plans. Treatment, suppression and community protection are complementary decisions at different scales. |
| 5. What does the evidence show? | Does treatment change what happens in a later wildfire? | A synthesis chart with uncertainty and a regional study-location map; then an Oregon example linking treatment history, severity and follow-up measurements. | Full studies, original metrics, sample sizes, intervals, limitations and overlap checks. Explain differences between an observed association, a modeled estimate and a causal estimate. |
| 6. What happens afterward? | Did the intended trees, habitat or community benefit? | Follow the same locations through ignition, initial assessment and later observations. Use separate traces for surviving trees, vegetation cover, habitat or invasive plants when data support them. | Field monitoring, case-specific RAVG/LCMS and dated photographs. A returning greenness signal is a vegetation observation, not a complete recovery measure. Include smoke and affected communities. |
| 7. What do our choices cost? | When does spending ahead help, and who pays? | A dated spending timeline, then alternative future branches and a sensitivity chart for expected costs/losses. Distinguish observed expenses from calculated scenarios. | Actual project costs, maintenance, incident reporting, exposure/encounter estimates and comparable outcomes. Public budget and broader social impacts require separate accounting. |
| 8. What can we do here? | What decisions can residents, land managers and governments make now? | A local place profile tying manager boundaries, documented priorities, nearby work, community protection, smoke information and public decisions together. | Current CWPPs, actual project documents, official action resources and participation opportunities. Show authority, constraints, funding and measurable goals. |

Transitions should do real explanatory work. Example: after showing reduced ladder fuels, ask “Does that change survive a real wildfire?” After examining a study, ask “How long does the effect last, and what does maintaining it cost?”

## 5. Cases should answer questions, with a published selection method

Use a case matrix: ecosystem, location, intervention, objective, evidence type, time since activity, community effects and missing records. Select across that matrix, including burdens, disappointing results or clear limits when documented. Documentary completeness is also a selection constraint; acknowledge the resulting availability bias in the reference methods.

Keep the existing cases, but give each a defined role:

- **Woodpecker:** choosing a location and ecological objectives, preparation and implementation. It is not currently a complete longitudinal or financial account.
- **Egley:** prior treatment, a later wildfire and one-/nine-year observations. Preserve the study's mixed treatment definitions.
- **Finley:** managing prairie and oak habitat, demonstrating that “forest remaining” is not the objective everywhere. Obtain individual burn and monitoring records before presenting a unit-level outcome.
- **A western-forest example:** local history and wildfire effects that test a dry-pine explanation's applicability. Select through records and scientific review, not visual drama.
- **A sagebrush example:** invasive annual grass, habitat and repeated-fire tradeoffs. Acquire an Oregon case with monitored outcomes.
- **A community example:** ember exposure, structure/parcel conditions and incident response. Forest treatment alone does not describe the whole community protection problem.

**New acquisition priority: Ashland Forest Resiliency as a possible recurring project.** The city's public site offers accomplishments/monitoring material and economic factsheets. That makes it a stronger candidate for connecting objectives, preparation, repeated work, water, community priorities and spending. This is a candidate, not a claim that all necessary data have been obtained or that its published accomplishments prove avoided loss. Inspect the monitoring plan, decisions, actual expenditures, unit histories, reporting periods and raw measurements before committing the guide to it.

Across chapters, return to a thoroughly documented project's geography, objective and timeline. Introduce contrasting cases where the same explanation no longer applies. Move the current case carousel into the reference collection; cases in the core guide appear at the point where they answer the chapter's question.

## 6. Numbers must support an explanation

Build a versioned factbook used by charts, reference entries and teacher materials. Each numerical claim needs: definition; value and units; location; period; population/denominator; source and page/table; method; uncertainty; exclusions; evidence type; calculation code where relevant; review state; and update/version information. Preserve reported values separately from derived values.

Priority numerical questions:

| Reader question | Product to build | Current evidence and next work |
|---|---|---|
| How much forest land does Oregon have, and what does that mean? | An area-context visual that separately explains forest land and canopy cover. | Existing verified FIA 2022 estimate: about 29.75 million acres. Preserve inventory date and sampling-error definition. The original PDF was not reachable through the web tool on this revisit; that is an access failure, not evidence it is missing. Reuse the existing verification and obtain the original for the factbook. |
| How much fire occurs, where and when? | Annual occurrence and area charts with explicit coverage, plus a map of the same defined population. | FPA FOD v7, 1992–2024, is the existing occurrence baseline. Reconcile 2025 and recent data before displaying a continuous series. Distinguish reported whole-incident area from mapped, Oregon-clipped footprint. Do not append unlike sources without reconciliation. |
| What kinds of prescribed work are being done? | Separate series for pile, broadcast and understory work, with definitions of acreage. | ODF 2025 report records 179,230 acres, 2,717 burned units and 3,941 ignitions. These are forestland-program measures and different counts, not a complete inventory of all Oregon burning. Inspect accomplishment tables and compare with returned unit/ignition records. Never chart the three figures as interchangeable burns. |
| How effective are treatments when wildfire reaches them? | A synthesis with method, uncertainty, study geography and treatment age; link to local cases. | Davis et al. 2024 reviewed 40 studies. No verified statewide avoided-cost estimate is available from the current atlas. |

Do not make the homepage's imported feature counts the headline statistics. A feature count is a database property; repeated treatments, overlapping systems, complexes, permits and accomplishments answer different questions.

### Numerical issue found in this audit: Egley

The published Results on page 7 report 26.7% (6,085 ha) of 23,226 untreated hectares and 12.9% (2,150 ha) of 19,233 treated hectares. Dividing the displayed hectares by those displayed totals gives approximately 26.2% and 11.2%, respectively. The discrepancy may reflect different denominators, exclusions or a publication error; it is not resolved here. The live guide uses the reported percentages, attributed to the study.

Before building a more prominent comparative chart or inferred acreage, inspect Figure 3, supporting data and any correction, and ask the authors to clarify the denominators. Keep the original percentages explicitly as reported values; do not silently replace them with recalculated values or imply the study's substantive result has been disproved. This illustrates why a source link alone is insufficient numerical verification.

## 7. The prevention-versus-response analysis

Start with one documented landscape and a consistent time horizon. Compare strategies that are ecologically and operationally feasible there. The baseline is the actual current-management strategy, including its response and maintenance activities; it is not an invented future with absolutely no action.

The reader first sees actual costs: planning, preparation, treatment, monitoring and repeat work. Next, explain that a future fire may or may not encounter the work while it remains effective. Finally, show which consequences the strategy could change: fire effects, response, property losses, smoke, water, habitat and other measured outcomes.

Use two accounts:

1. **Public and private spending:** costs to each payer, with timing, funding sources and discounting for scenarios.
2. **Benefits and burdens:** property and health losses where estimable, plus separately displayed ecological/community measures. Do not add nonmonetary scores to dollars.

For a narrow teaching example, show:

`expected avoided loss = probability of a relevant encounter × change in loss if it occurs`

For the actual decision analysis, calculate the difference in discounted expected costs and losses between strategies over the same horizon, including maintenance, effectiveness over time, multiple possible fire outcomes, treatment adverse effects and benefits that occur without a later wildfire. Use scenario ranges and identify which assumptions can change the conclusion. Avoid double counting response costs or monetized outcomes.

The existing hypothetical slider can teach the arithmetic in the classroom/reference section. It cannot serve as the empirical economic conclusion. Local probability, loss reduction and project costs must be estimated separately with public evidence and methodological review.

Thompson et al. 2017's Sierra National Forest model found median suppression savings of zero despite potential gains in extreme years. This is useful for explaining probability and the difference between annual experience and expected value. Its California parameters are not Oregon inputs. Bayham et al. 2022 provides an economics literature framework; its catalog abstract was inspected here, not a completed extraction of all estimates.

Prioritize an actual cost account, a defensible break-even calculation and a sensitivity plot. Do not promise a local return-on-investment estimate if counterfactual loss information remains insufficient. Label measured, literature-estimated and hypothetical values clearly at the point of use.

## 8. School use is a separate design and validation task

Start with grades 6–8 and a high-school extension. Do not claim a single adult essay meets every grade's needs. Adapt the evidence and language for younger students later with educators.

The USFS FireWorks program already offers regional curricula. Its Southern Oregon Fire Ecology Education version includes K–2, 3–5 and 6–8 materials, lesson-time/standards overview charts, and partnership-developed Indigenous knowledge content. OSU's Oregon Natural Resources Education Program is a relevant educator-review partner. Obtain applicable reuse permissions; do not imply those programs endorse this atlas.

Proposed five-lesson classroom sequence:

| Lesson | Student work | Deliverables |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Explain the mechanism | Draw and explain a path from surface fuel to trees or buildings; change one condition and predict qualitatively. | Diagram, vocabulary, teacher notes and misconception check. |
| 2. Compare places and histories | Compare two Oregon landscapes using sources, dates and local context. | Map excerpts, dated images, reading packet and questions about what the evidence supports. |
| 3. Read a real study | Compare treated/untreated observations, distinguish percentages from percentage points and identify the study's limits. | Reviewed chart, small data table, spreadsheet/CSV and answer key. |
| 4. Evaluate a decision | Compare feasible actions against stated goals, constraints, costs and uncertainty. | Project packet, simple expected-value exercise and stakeholder perspectives. |
| 5. Make and defend a proposal | Recommend actions for a defined place, cite evidence and identify the observations needed to check outcomes. | Claim/evidence/reasoning rubric and pre-/post-learning questions. |

Oregon's adopted 2022 science standards integrate practices, concepts and disciplinary ideas. Candidate alignment inspected: 6.ESS3.3 (monitor/minimize environmental impacts), HS.LS2.6 (evaluate evidence about ecosystem change), HS.LS2.7 (evaluate/refine environmental solutions). A teacher must review actual task-to-standard alignment; matching a topic name is not sufficient. Use the state's actual identifiers, not only generic NGSS labels.

Consult ODE Tribal History/Shared History science materials and partners for historical/contemporary stewardship, sovereignty and land-management context. Publicly available curricula do not authorize reuse of restricted local knowledge.

Provide printable lessons, teacher answers, captioned media, accessible data tables, keyboard use, reduced-motion support, a no-animation path and a low-bandwidth/offline packet. Do not require student accounts or location disclosure. Teach with approved paper/digital activities; no unsupervised ignition activities.

Pilot with teachers and learners from several settings, including a fire-affected community. Measure whether students can transfer the concepts to an unfamiliar place, not just recall vocabulary. Gather which passages and visuals cause misunderstanding. Offer a Spanish-language edition after review of terminology and translation quality.

## 10. Visual direction and language

Keep the forest-green, cream and editorial typography. Establish visual hierarchy with large readable diagrams, restrained explanatory annotations and generous but purposeful space. Photographs establish place; diagrams explain mechanisms; charts quantify comparisons; maps explain location and scale. Every visual must answer a question.

Use consistent legends for planned activity, completed activity, mechanical preparation, wildfire extent and observations. Differentiate color with patterns/labels. Charts use compatible units, honest axes and uncertainty appropriate to their methods. No animated acres ticker suggests statewide ecological success.

Essential ideas are visible in the reading flow. Interaction reveals an answer, isolates a condition or lets the visitor inspect evidence; it does not replace explanation. Core paragraphs should explain a concrete mechanism or consequence in familiar language. Define technical terms at first use. Keep dates, credits and meaningful scope beside evidence; put lengthy methods behind a clear link. Remove repeated workflow apologies and abstract headings that do not tell the reader what happens.

## 11. First implementation sequence

1. **Write and review the core lesson before assembling the page.** Draft the eight-chapter narrative and transitions; build a claim list; test whether it works as a plain document. Freeze the source/evidence needs for each visual.
2. **Storyboard three connected chapters first:** mechanism, different places and choices. Build a first-minute prototype and test it with people unfamiliar with fire. They should explain why one solution does not apply everywhere before seeing record filters.
3. **Complete the first quantitative evidence package:** ODF program activity definitions, reviewed treatment synthesis, one Oregon follow-up study and a continuous-series coverage audit. Resolve the Egley denominator issue before a prominent new chart. Acquire intervals/supplements for the synthesis.
4. **Acquire a complete recurring project packet, starting with the Ashland candidate.** Verify geography, dates, activities, goals, costs, monitoring, images and permissions. Integrate contrasting cases by defined teaching role.
5. **Build the actual spending account and the transparent decision analysis.** Publish the level supported by available evidence; retain missing inputs as explicit research gaps rather than zeros.
6. **Restructure the main page and reference routes.** Preserve old links and existing map states, improve glossary/factbook navigation and separate the reference apparatus from the lesson.
7. **Produce and pilot classroom materials.** Scientific, economic, teacher and appropriate cultural review; accessible/print/offline versions; revise from comprehension testing.

No new outreach was sent during this audit. Reconcile prior requests before preparing new packets; outbound outreach remains subject to the shared email policy and gate. The private interview and claim ledger remain excluded from public evidence and build inputs.

## 12. What counts as a successful release

- A first-time reader can explain surface fire, canopy fire and embers; identify why landscape/conditions matter; and distinguish a mapped boundary from measured effects.
- A reader can describe one project's objective, preparation, spending and documented outcome, with the missing evidence apparent.
- The reader can explain why severity reduction is not the same as fires prevented or money saved.
- Each headline number has a reproducible definition, population, units, period, source/locator and reviewed calculation.
- The main lesson remains coherent on mobile, with JavaScript/animation disabled and without functioning map services.
- Existing exploration URLs retain their states; teacher versions and printed charts retain citations, units and essential explanations.
- A scientific reviewer, an economics reviewer, teachers and novice readers have tested the relevant content. Review attribution does not imply institutional endorsement.
- Evidence corrections and updates have visible version history; source counts are never presented as unqualified statewide fire totals.
