# Portland's small-business economy: research dossier

**Evidence cutoff: September 30, 2026.** Central question: *What would make Portland a better place to build a sustainable business, and would doing so improve life for owners, workers, customers and residents?* Here “Portland” is the city for policy; every county or metro statistic is labeled. This is a research foundation, with 68 registered sources and 16 reproducible figures. It is not yet a representative account of city firms or a completed 150–250-source review. Confidence labels reflect the evidence available here.

## The picture that can be drawn now

The strongest **city-specific** published time series found so far is Prosper Portland’s [2025 Insights & Indicators, Figure 1.06](https://prosperportland.us/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Insights-Indicators-2025.pdf). It reports **108,785 “small-business” jobs in incorporated Portland in 2019 and 112,411 in 2024**, a **3.3%** net increase, with the Central City lagging the citywide recovery. It cites Oregon QCEW. However, the report calls the size group **1–20 employees** on its chart page and **1–19** elsewhere; standard QCEW size tabulations are establishment-based, while the prose says “firms.” The underlying city extract and size unit are not supplied. This is a valuable **reported city series**, not yet a compatible city share of all jobs or directly comparable to the Census enterprise-size numbers below. **Confidence: moderate for the reported endpoints; unresolved for size-unit comparability.** [Claims c35–c36](claims.tsv)

In the **Portland–Vancouver–Hillsboro metro**, the revised [2022 Census SUSB file](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/susb/datasets/2022/msa_3digitnaics_2022.txt) records 1,084,535 employer payroll jobs. Enterprises with fewer than 20 employees held 203,569, **18.8%**; enterprises with fewer than 500 held 544,855, **50.2%**. The under-500 group accounted for **41.0% of payroll** and **35.4% of receipts**. These are enterprise-size categories and metro figures; no city GDP claim follows from them. The nonoverlapping size bands, denominator and original noise flags are in [the extract](data/metro-size-2022.csv). **Confidence: high for the published measures, not for city extrapolation.** [Claims c01–c05](claims.tsv)

In **Multnomah County**, the separate 2022 SUSB workbook records 439,591 employer jobs; 216,121, or **49.2%**, are at enterprises under 500 and 76,485, or **17.4%**, under 20. The county is not a Portland city proxy without bias: it includes places outside city limits and misses Portland's other county portions. The 2023 Census Nonemployer Statistics file counts **77,829** tax-reporting businesses without paid employees in the county, with **$4.272 billion in gross receipts**. This is evidence that a huge part of the business landscape is missing from employer-only charts, but does not measure owner take-home pay or sustainable full-time livelihoods. The years, populations and addresses differ, so the two datasets are not added. **Confidence: high for source totals; low for livelihood inference.** [County SUSB](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/susb/tables/2022/county_3digitnaics_2022.xlsx), [NES methods](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/nonemployer-statistics/technical-documentation/methodology.html)

The popular image of local business as mainly restaurants and shops is incomplete. In 2022 metro SUSB, **health care had 165,698 employer jobs**, retail 121,817, manufacturing 113,841, accommodation/food 100,485, professional services 85,883, and construction 82,002. Professional services had 8,870 employer firms and construction 8,366; health care had 7,083 and food/accommodation 5,217. County nonemployers were led by professional services (15,372), transport (10,637), arts/recreation (9,131), health care (7,373) and real estate (7,043). These are different universes, and a sector total includes both small and large firms. **Confidence: high for composition, limited for city industry mix.** [Metro sectors](data/metro-sectors-2022.csv), [county nonemployers](data/nonemployer-sectors-2023.csv)

## Where Portland may shine, and where it may not

The 2022 size mix makes Portland relatively small-firm-intensive among **eight named peer metros**. Under-500 employment share was 50.2% versus Sacramento 50.4%, Austin 48.1%, Minneapolis–St. Paul 45.8%, Denver 45.2%, Pittsburgh 44.9%, Seattle 43.9%, Indianapolis 42.7% and Salt Lake City 42.0%. For under-20 firms, Portland's 18.8% was behind Sacramento's 19.0% and above the other listed peers. This is a descriptive ordering among selected metros, not an index of success, productivity, entrepreneurship quality, or policy causality. Metro boundaries and industry composition vary. The under-500 category is much broader than the Chamber's 1–50-employee definition. **Confidence: high for shares; low for a “best city” interpretation.** [Peer extract](data/metro-size-2022.csv)

A pre-pandemic comparison changes the narrative. [BLS QCEW](https://www.bls.gov/cew/overview.htm) private covered annual-average employment in Multnomah County fell from **447,067 in 2019 to 415,659 in 2025** (−7.0%). Health care rose from 67,288 to 75,064 (+11.6%), while manufacturing fell from 35,565 to 28,155 (−20.8%), food/accommodation from 51,021 to 43,456 (−14.8%), and retail from 42,841 to 37,024 (−13.6%). Professional services fell modestly from 40,624 to 39,370. These trajectories indicate different sector pressures; they do **not** isolate firm size, business survival, Portland city, or the effect of local government. Health care growth can reflect population need, consolidation or wages as well as entrepreneurial opportunity. Manufacturing contraction may hide productive surviving firms. A sector concentration measure is not sector performance. **Confidence: high for QCEW broad trends, low for causes.** [Extract](data/qcew-sectors-2019-2025.csv)

## Twelve publication questions and current answers

| Question | Current answer and competing explanations | What would resolve it |
|---|---|---|
| What firms do we have? | Employer metro and county structure and county nonemployers are measured. City-level firms by age, ownership, franchise status and neighborhood are not. A license or state registration count would overstate active firms. | City-safe aggregates, geocoded Census/administrative access, ABS/NESD ownership tables, sector interviews. |
| Where do we shine? | Small-enterprise job share is high in this selected peer set; professional and health sectors are substantial. High share could reflect opportunity, industry mix, fragmentation or fewer large anchors. | National metro distribution, industry-standardized shares, age/entry measures, wages and survival. |
| How much activity is small vs large? | 2022 metro employer `<500`: 50.2% jobs, 41.0% payroll, 35.4% receipts. City GDP split is unresolved. These three measures do not imply owner profit. | Size-by-industry value-added study or acceptable model; see [GDP memo](gdp-feasibility.md). |
| Do owners earn a decent living? | Unknown locally. NES receipts cannot answer expenses, unpaid hours, family support, benefits or income volatility. A high business count can coexist with precarious self-employment. | Voluntary financial/time-use diaries and tax-safe aggregates, with stratified sampling and failed-firm interviews. |
| Are jobs good? | Metro payroll per mid-March job differs by size, but industry and worker composition confound it. Local firm-size data on benefits, schedules, advancement and safety are absent. | Within-NAICS matched payroll/benefit comparisons, worker interviews, UI wage records where permitted. |
| Are firms durable? | The BDS metro extract shows establishment entry/exit and 2023 firm-age groups, but not city cohort survival. A firm growing past a “small” threshold is success, not failure. | BDS metro age-by-size tables and city administrative cohorts, with right-censoring and relocation separated from closure. |
| Who gets ownership opportunity? | Current program and sector sources cannot establish population-level reach by race, gender, disability, immigration, language or geography. | ABS/NESD where geography allows, program eligibility and outcome denominators, disparity study when complete, privacy-protected city aggregates. |
| What holds businesses back? | Reports identify permits, premises, capital, customers, staffing and administrative work, but reported frustrations do not yet rank measured cost. Business type matters: a home care provider and restaurant face different regulators and demand. | Permit cohorts and time-to-open cases; retail/office/industrial space cost evidence; representative owner instrument. |
| Does support reach the right people? | OSB reports 759 unique businesses served and a food-heavy client mix (33% food, 15% retail/storefront). No eligible-population denominator or nonuser funnel is available. | De-duplicated program records from awareness to resolution, linked to eligibility and neighborhood, plus nonuser sampling. |
| Is spending sufficient and effective? | Cannot answer from contact counts or proposed budget. Actual operating spend, grants, loan principal, guarantees and overlapping programs need separate accounting; marginal outcomes need a comparator. | Actual ledgers, vendor contracts, referral IDs, decisions, case closure and outcome data, then pilot evaluation. |
| Do small and large firms depend on each other? | Plausible channels include supplier purchases, anchor demand, subcontracting, talent and customers. No quantified local inter-firm flow is in this package. More local sales can displace another local firm. | Procurement/invoice aggregates, input-output context, anchor interviews, consumer and supplier mapping. |
| What should future support do? | The strongest near-term design hypothesis is measurable service resolution, then tested shared services and process reform. AI can reduce drafting and search time but cannot create customers, space or grant budgets. | Three costed pilots with comparator and human review; [policy comparison](future-program.md). |

## Business churn and age

The newly extracted [Census BDS metro table](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/bds/tables/time-series/2023/bds2023_msa.csv) reports **6,911 establishment entries and 6,588 exits in 2023** for the Portland MSA. Entry/exit rates were **10.49% and 10.00%**, respectively, after a 2022 entry-rate spike to 11.84% and exit rate of 9.53%. These are establishments, not unique new or dead firms; a chain opening a site counts as entry. The [age-zero firm category](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/bds/tables/time-series/2023/bds2023_msa_fac.csv) contains **4,472 new employer firms** in 2023. The age table also has 14,332 firms aged 1–5, 9,345 aged 6–10, 22,174 aged 11+ and 3,275 in an older left-censored category. These totals reconcile to the BDS metro firm count of 53,598, but are not comparable to SUSB 2022 counts because year and construction differ. Nothing here measures the survival probability of a Portland city startup cohort, and the pandemic-era rates should not be read as a smooth trend. **Confidence: high for BDS published values, unresolved for city durability.** [BDS methodology](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/bds/documentation/methodology.html), [extracts](data/bds-metro-2019-2023.csv)

## Why a small-business share is not itself a goal

“Small” mixes a successful independent manufacturer with a part-time sole proprietor and a struggling storefront. Business age matters: [Haltiwanger, Jarmin and Miranda](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16300) show that young firms, rather than small size alone, account for a central part of the apparent size/job-growth relationship in national data. That is a warning against rewarding firms simply for staying below a size cap. Successful policy could raise survival **and** let firms graduate into larger classes. Equally, a high survival rate could result from weak entry, while high entry could represent low-quality necessity entrepreneurship. Portland's selected-peer rank cannot adjudicate these cases. Measure durable owner income, worker quality, useful output and opportunity alongside starts, exits and size shares.

Payroll per job across 2022 metro firm sizes generally differs markedly: the under-500 group averaged about $57,361 annual payroll per mid-March job, while the 500-plus complement is higher. This unadjusted arithmetic is **not** evidence that moving a given worker to a large firm would raise pay: industries, occupations, hours and the denominator differ. Within-industry, within-occupation data and benefits are needed. Aggregate small-firm wage gaps should not be excused because a business is beloved; nor should a firm be disfavored simply because it grew.

## Does current support work?

The [Office of Small Business Year One report](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6801755ad7acc40d46586c99/t/6aa09dc0156a0f27de3f3e85/1788911040812/OSB+Year+One+Impact+Report+Accessible+1.pdf) documents first-year activity and stories. It reports **759 unique businesses**; 50% of service classifications were “Prosper Portland resources,” 18% community connections, 14% other resources, 11% city bureau support and 7% capital access. Inquiry origins were 30% liaison outreach, 25% appointment, 19% Prosper website, 10% phone, 9% OSB website, 5% partner referral, 2% chat. The report gives both **1,600 touchpoints** and **1,700-plus interactions**, without defining a crosswalk. Its four district business counts (103, 182, 154, 142) sum to **581**, 178 short of the 759 citywide unique count. The discrepancy may involve missing location data, different periods or definitions; none is established. The right next step is de-duplicated aggregate records and reconciliation, not an invented correction. These are **outputs**, not evidence of increased owner income or net jobs. **Confidence: moderate for reported outputs; impact unresolved.**

Prosper's [IBRN FY2023–24 board update](https://prosperportland.us/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Report-25-11.pdf) reports **621 businesses served** and around **30% client outcome-survey response**. Its clients may overlap with OSB, and its period differs, so the two reach figures cannot be summed. The [City Auditor's 2021 emergency-grant audit](https://www.portland.gov/auditor/audit-services/news/2021/5/12/prosper-portland-emergency-grants-standards-needed-ensure) found weak standards and a 36% outcome-survey response for the **2020 emergency grants**, limiting their outcome assessment. It is an accountability precedent, **not a finding that OSB failed**. Current services may solve real cases; the present public evidence cannot estimate how often, for whom, at what cost, or compared with simpler city processes. Avoid both blanket praise and blanket dismissal.

A [FY2025–26 Prosper budget committee report](https://www.portland.gov/council/documents/report/placed-file/2025-209) gives a **proposed agency-wide $189 million and 105 positions**, spanning real estate, workforce, traded sectors, small firms and more. It sets targets of $23 million in new loans for 15 small businesses **and urban-development projects**, $7.3 million in grants for 125 businesses through several programs, and 660 unique IBRN clients. These are **proposal targets**, not actual small-business expenditure or verified outcomes. Loan principal should not be added to operating grants as if both were costs. This is evidence of material public effort and an accounting problem, not a basis for saying support is too much or too little. [Claims c33–c34](claims.tsv)

The [Chamber 2023 report](https://portlandmetrochamber.com/resources/2023-state-of-small-business/) defines small as **1–50 employees** and gives apparently conflicting **20% and 28%** metro small-business employment shares in different sections. A [Council acceptance record](https://www.portland.gov/council/documents/report/accepted/283-2024) repeats 28%. These numbers need original tables, denominator and erratum; they cannot be reconciled by comparing to Census `<20` or `<500` shares. This unresolved conflict matters because it affects the baseline policymakers may cite.

## Publication and reporting priorities

1. **Publish now with verified scale and caveats:** a visual first chapter on enterprise size and industry with side-by-side jobs, payroll and receipts; show metro and county separately, plus the selected-peer benchmark. Lead with what the measures mean and omit a GDP percentage. Figures 1–11 and 16 support this chapter.
2. **Investigate the city's actual service journey:** request anonymized eligible population, applications, decisions, referrals, completed resolutions, elapsed time, spending and client follow-up; reconcile OSB and Chamber numbers. Figures 12–15 become an “activity versus outcomes” chapter, not an impact claim.
3. **Report owner/worker lived economics:** purposeful, diverse interviews on income after expenses, hours, pay, scheduling, customer demand and tradeoffs. Interview closed, relocated, denied and never-served firms. Treat interviews as mechanisms and cases, not prevalence estimates.
4. **Test the alternatives:** compare a smoother permit/payment process, coordinated casework, and shared human/AI assistance with matched or randomized rollout where feasible. Define success before procurement. Publish cost and worker/customer effects, including whether gain is displacement or new value.
5. **Revisit GDP only if warranted:** a specialist size-by-industry value-added study may be costly and still wide-interval. It should not crowd out direct measures of owner income and worker quality.

The gap inventory and unsent records-request/interview drafts are in [the fieldwork kit](fieldwork-kit.md) and [priority gaps](notes/gaps-and-requests.md). This first pass does not claim that source discovery is exhausted: the register identifies BDS age-by-size extension, ABS and formation work, and the crawl log names inaccessible pages. Further bibliography-chaining and two consecutive low-yield search rounds are still needed before declaring topical saturation.
