# Public understanding: proposed next iteration — September 12, 2026

The project owner approved this direction. See [the implementation and evidence notes](public-guide-release-2026-09-12.md) for the completed public-guide iteration and remaining acquisition needs. The existing map provides records, recent fire boundaries, severity, and forest-area context. The next priority is helping residents interpret those records through place, ecology, concrete examples, tradeoffs, and evidence of outcomes.

## Recommended order

1. **Start with a place.** Search a town, watershed, or familiar park. Present a concise, sourced explanation of its vegetation, mapped fire history, management responsibility, nearby documented projects, and uncertainties. Avoid implying a parcel-level risk assessment. Generate summaries from reviewed facts and verified spatial intersections rather than unreviewed inferred narratives.
2. **Offer a short guided introduction.** Explain Oregon's different fire regimes using linked landscape photographs and map scenes. Distinguish perimeter, vegetation change, and ecological outcomes. Historical fire regimes provide context rather than a universal present-day burn schedule. Include climate, weather, fire exclusion, land use, and invasive species where supported locally.
3. **Build three documented stories.** Begin with Woodpecker after obtaining verified geometry; acquire a studied Oregon treatment/wildfire encounter; develop a prairie, oak, or cultural-fire story with a willing partner. Each needs purpose, before/after images, dated observations, outcomes, shortcomings, sources, and a human explanation. Cultural-fire content should be developed with the relevant tribal partners, including decisions about precise locations and attribution.
4. **Show recovery through time.** Same-season imagery and repeat ground photographs before fire, shortly after, and at later observations. Keep regeneration, canopy change, severity, habitat effects, and ecological success distinct. Greenness alone is not proof of recovery. Include unassessed and mixed outcomes.
5. **Explain why planned burns wait.** A seasonal calendar and reviewed example explaining weather, fuel moisture, smoke, staffing, preparation, and approvals. A missing feed record does not establish cancellation; project-specific delay reasons require documentation. Any interactive conditions demonstration must be labeled illustrative rather than an operational go/no-go tool.
6. **Make choices and accountability visible.** For a pilot landscape, compare documented objectives, proposed alternatives, implementation, cost where comparable, monitoring, and public participation opportunities. Prescribed fire, thinning with subsequent burning, suppression, and management of naturally ignited fire depend on place and conditions. Identify benefits, burdens, uncertainty, and whose values define success. Do not equate lower vegetation severity with homes protected or universal ecological benefit.
7. **Make smoke and daily relevance legible.** Explain smoke tradeoffs and connect to official current observations and protective guidance. Preserve the distinction between nearby fire, measured ambient smoke, and established source attribution. Keep emergency information visibly separate from historical analysis.

## Design direction

Use a clear opening choice between a guided introduction, finding a place, and exploring records. Lead with recognizable landscapes and concrete questions. Let story steps update one map, with direct routes back to free exploration. Offer a short plain-language takeaway, then evidence and uncertainty on demand. Avoid autoplay, compulsory scroll sequences, unexplained legends, and treating missing data as zero. Keep mobile, keyboard, and shareable scene URLs integral.

The first implementation slice should be the guided introduction, place-based entry, and one verified story. A statewide “good fire” percentage remains inappropriate until geography, ecological objectives, time window, overlap rules, and outcome evidence are defined. A useful pilot dashboard would keep completed work, monitored outcomes, and community impacts separate.

## Sources checked

- [OSU: The Ecological Effects of Fire](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/pub/em-9340-ecological-effects-fire) — Oregon's different fire regimes and ecological effects.
- [OSU: Prescribed fire — Why we burn](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/em-9339-prescribed-fire-why-we-burn) — regional management purposes and cultural burning.
- [USFS: How do thinning, prescribed fire, and wildfire affect future wildfire severity?](https://research.fs.usda.gov/rmrs/articles/how-do-thinning-prescribed-fire-and-wildfire-affect-future-wildfire-severity) — treatment evidence and the importance of treatment type and elapsed time; not a guarantee for an individual event.
- [USFS: Egley Fire Complex treatment study](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/59149) — Oregon case-study acquisition lead; study catalog checked, supporting dataset and site geometry not acquired in this pass.
- [OSU: Fire Weather](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/em-9385-prescribed-fire-basics-fire-weather) — weather and fuel conditions influence feasible burn windows.
- [OSU: Planning a Prescribed Burn](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/pub/em-9343-planning-prescribed-burn) — objectives, resources, smoke, contingencies, and monitoring.
- [OSU: Smoke management](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/em-9342-smoke-management) — smoke-sensitive areas and management objectives.
- [OSU: Air-quality impacts from prescribed fire and wildfire](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/em-9203-fire-faqs-air-quality-impacts-prescribed-fire-wildfire) — smoke harms and comparison context.

No new interviews, outreach, imagery licensing, or ecological review were completed in this proposal pass.
