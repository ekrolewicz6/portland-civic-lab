# Production, workshops, and routes to customers

*Public evidence reviewed September 15, 2026. These are contrasting examples, not a representative sample. No interviews, financial verification, or site visits were conducted. “Unknown” means not established in the reviewed material.*

For every case, distinguish **documented output**, **advertised capacity**, and **economic results still to measure**. The full discovery inventory includes additional networks and workshops in [ecosystem.csv](data/ecosystem.csv).

## 1. Past Lives: shared production across materials

**Role and place:** multidisciplinary makerspace, 2808 SE 9th Avenue. The operator describes wood, metal, textile, glass, and prototyping facilities. Its portfolio and descriptions identify furniture, metal objects, artistic commissions, and industrial support. The homepage reports a facility of 26,000 square feet, 55 private studios, and 176 completed commissions; periods and utilization are unspecified. [Facilities and portfolio](https://www.pastlives.space/home)

**Production chain:** access to shared equipment → fabrication by members or the design/build team → objects, commissions, or prototype services → clients and collectors. The links are documented as services or portfolio activity; revenues and the allocation of production between members and the operator are not disclosed.

**Access:** published membership terms include $200 monthly with a minimum three-month contract, $180 monthly under a prepaid annual contract, or $250 for a single month. Orientations cost extra. Those terms cannot be combined with an undated membership count to calculate receipts. [Membership terms](https://www.pastlives.space/membership)

**What to learn next:** unique active users, businesses earning income, completed work during one year, contract values, maker versus operator receipts, and local subcontracting. Ask which commissions originated through the space and which would have happened elsewhere.

**Source tension:** the homepage describes retail sales while the membership page labels a retail store as forthcoming. Treat current retail operation as unresolved. Listed classes show offerings, not course completions or realized teaching income. [Class listings](https://classes.pastlives.space/)

## 2. ADX: studios, shared woodworking, and routes to audiences

**Role and place:** artist-studio collective and makerspace, 417 SE 11th Avenue. The studios page reports a community exceeding 95 creatives across physical and nonphysical disciplines, with exhibition and market opportunities. The reporting period and count of active income-earning makers are unknown. [Studios](https://artdesignxchange.com/art-studios-portland)

**Production chain:** studio and tool access → member work → exhibitions, markets, or direct commissions. Public evidence establishes infrastructure and disciplines. It does not provide an inventory of finished goods, sales, or jobs attributable to the space.

**Access:** co-making access is advertised at $75 monthly. Woodshop access is a distinct offer: $160 monthly with a yearlong contract, demonstrated tool proficiency, and a membership limit of 20 woodworkers. The limit is capacity, not current enrollment. The woodshop explicitly does not cater to beginners. [Co-making](https://artdesignxchange.com/communitymembership) · [Woodshop](https://artdesignxchange.com/woodshop-portland)

**What to learn next:** occupied studios, unique producers, outside clients, annual sales through events, and businesses sharing staff or referrals. Separate nonphysical creative work from the maker subset without treating it as less valuable. Compare access bundles, including training and storage, rather than ranking spaces by a single advertised price.

## 3. Morning Ceramics: a workshop that also serves production at home

**Role and place:** specialized ceramics workshop, 239 SE 6th Avenue. The operator lists wheels, handbuilding equipment, and kilns, with membership tiers based partly on storage space. One-shelf community membership is advertised at $195 monthly; firing is additional. [Membership](https://www.morningceramics.com/membership)

**Production chain:** clay and shaping → shared or home workspace → firing → ceramic objects → markets or other customers. Outside firing and wheel rental explicitly serve people working away from the studio. A member-only count would therefore omit some production supported by this facility. The market archive documents recurring sales events, not transactions or vendor earnings. [Services](https://www.morningceramics.com/services) · [Markets](https://www.morningceramics.com/markets)

**Unknown economics:** active users, firing volume, market receipts, the commercial share of production, and customer geography. A firing count could be a useful utilization measure, but one kiln load is not a person, business, or completed sale.

**What to learn next:** separately count members, outside-firing customers, wheel renters, instructors, and event vendors, then identify overlaps. Ask producers about unsold stock, paid working hours, and whether home and shared facilities are substitutes or complements.

## 4. PDX Hackerspace: technical experimentation and possible contract work

**Role and place:** technical community workshop, 7608 N Interstate Avenue, also known as CTRL+H. Its public site describes electronics, craft, and shop facilities. [Facilities](https://pdxhackerspace.org/)

**Production chain:** tools and technical collaboration → experimental devices, repairs, or project work → sharing or occasional paid contracts. Its FAQ describes matching community project requests with members; it does not report the number, value, or completion of those contracts. Membership is advertised at $40 monthly, with some additional equipment, class, and material fees. [FAQ](https://pdxhackerspace.org/FAQ.html)

DorkbotPDX meets at the same site and reports typical attendance of 20–50 people, with no defined averaging period. Its subject matter includes electronics and experimental technology. Those attendances must not become a second site's membership count. [Dorkbot](https://dorkbotpdx.org/)

**What to learn next:** documented completed prototypes, subsequent paid work, equipment utilization, repair outcomes, and technical skills used elsewhere. Separate hobby experimentation, open technical contributions, and commercial production. The absence of sales does not erase participation value, but it prevents an unsupported revenue estimate.

## 5. Portland Garment Factory: commissioned fabrication

**Production and place:** 311 SE 97th Avenue. PGF documents garments, installations, costumes, and fabricated artwork for commercial and institutional clients. Its portfolio includes OMSI lab coats, Portland Japanese Garden uniforms, retail displays, and furniture collaboration. These are documented project types, not an audited current order book. [Portfolio](https://www.portlandgarmentfactory.com/)

**A completed project:** a Nike Portland flagship installation transformed reclaimed sports jerseys and soccer balls into a large garment. The credits distinguish Britt Howard's design, PGF's build, and patches and chainstitching by Alex Stone and Stephenie Goodwin. That makes the division of skilled work visible even though the payment record remains private. [Project credits](https://www.portlandgarmentfactory.com/our-work-1/retail-installation-nike-flagship)

**Economic route:** client brief → design and sourcing → specialized fabrication and finishing → installation. Count the client's final purchase separately from subcontractor receipts to avoid calling both new final demand. Whether any particular collaborator was an employee or independent contractor is not established by a credit.

**Next evidence:** contract value, work hours by location, purchased inputs, subcontractor payments, and client geography. A large advertised production capability is not evidence of delivered volume.

## 6. Rachael Potter Ceramics: making beyond shared-space membership

**Role and place:** a ceramic business whose operator describes working from a home studio and buying clay and glaze locally in Portland. A city-limits production location is not independently established; the private address is neither sought nor published here. [Operator description](https://www.rachaelpotter.com/)

**Production chain:** locally purchased materials → wheel-thrown or handbuilt pottery at home → online customers. The shop offers domestic and international shipping, but that does not establish completed outside sales. Neither annual receipts nor the number of people working in the business is public in the reviewed source.

**What to learn next:** business versus supplemental income, working time, materials and firing expenses, customer locations, and use of shared services. No relationship to Morning Ceramics is asserted.

This case explains a sampling problem: recruiting only through makerspaces misses unaffiliated home producers. Public portfolios can help discover businesses, but membership, tax records, and web visibility each reveal different parts of the population.

## 7. Orox Leather: one producer, several ways to reach customers

Orox describes its goods as handmade in Portland and identifies a Chinatown workshop at 450 NW Couch Street plus an airport retail location. Its leather goods therefore connect a production site to distinct customer-facing locations. Do not multiply the enterprise count by its number of stores. [Public locations](https://www.oroxleather.com/pages/visit-us)

**Economic route:** leather and hardware → workshop fabrication → direct retail or online sale. The public storefront information does not establish where inputs originated, employee counts, sales volumes, or how much airport spending comes from outside Portland. Ask for channel-level sales, production employment, local purchases, repair work, and customer geography in aggregate.

## 8. The Joinery: a visible regional material relationship

The Joinery's December 2, 2025 account documents a modular table built in Portland for CityTeam. Zena Forest Products donated white oak from the Willamette Valley; the table was also donated. The design lets a large gathering table become smaller tables. The company identifies its builder and explains that the finished work was signed. [Project account](https://thejoinery.com/blogs/news/brining-oregon-lumber-and-craftsmanship-to-city-team)

**Economic route:** regional timber → material preparation and skilled construction → a finished object used by a Portland organization. Because this was a donation, it does not supply a contract price. It does supply a traceable example of material sourcing and local fabrication. A comparable paid project would let us examine labor, materials, freight, overhead, and retained income without guessing a furniture-industry multiplier.

## 9. IPRC: print production and book arts

The Independent Publishing Resource Center's studios at 318 SE Main Street include letterpress and other print-making resources. Its studio page describes access and training, including a $50 orientation that includes one month of membership, with a waiver option. This is an introductory offer, not a continuing monthly price. [Studios and access](https://www.iprc.org/the-studios)

**Economic route:** paper, ink, layout, and equipment access → printed objects and publications → direct sales, commissions, or noncommercial circulation. Public facilities establish capacity; print runs, paid editions, and annual maker earnings require project records. Track workshops and volunteer hours separately from paid production.

## 10. Radius: studio access, firing, and home equipment

Radius at 2324 SE Belmont Street offers hourly, monthly, and at-home access. Its monthly one-shelf option is $150 with an initial six-month commitment; firing costs extra. The current membership page advertises a community of 70-plus working artists without defining the reporting period. [Membership terms](https://www.radiusstudio.org/membership/)

**Economic route:** shaping clay in the studio or at home → drying, glazing, and firing → finished ceramics → sale or personal use. Storage, kiln access, and scheduling are distinct constraints. To understand productive capacity, measure members, unique firing customers, loads, waiting times, and commercial versus personal use separately. A firing bill can establish a purchased production service; it does not establish a sale of the finished piece.

## 11. NW Marine Art Works: a concentration of studios

At 2516 NW 29th Avenue, NW Marine Art Works occupies former Northwest Marine Ironworks buildings. The operator describes 80 studios and more than 100 artists and makers. Those are undated operator figures, not equivalent counts of businesses, workers, or paid producers. [Site history and studios](https://www.nwmarineartworks.com/about)

The economic questions are studio occupancy, rents, lease stability, commercial disciplines, and which production would move or end if space became unavailable. The concentration makes recruitment easier, but should not stand in for dispersed home studios or less visible work elsewhere in Portland.

## 12. Technical, neighborhood, and volunteer infrastructure

**Hedron Hackerspace**, at 2020 SE Bush Street, advertises 3D printers, laser cutters, and welding among its shared tools. The website demonstrates an equipment-and-community offer, not the count of commercial prototypes delivered. [Hedron](https://hhacker.space/)

**Workshop St. Johns**, at 6635 N Baltimore Avenue, describes artist and artisan studios in the former Columbia Sportswear property. **St. Johns Clay Collective** lists the same street address and offers ceramic instruction and open studio use. Record a property and a hosted program as related entries, not two independent populations. [Workshop property](https://workshopstjohns.com/) · [Clay Collective](https://www.stjohnsclay.com/)

**Guild of Oregon Woodworkers** describes a volunteer-run organization with more than 1,100 members and no employees, without a measurement year. General dues are $85 per year; shop access is separately priced at $15 per hour. Membership includes hobbyists and professionals, and reaches beyond Portland. This is a particularly clear example of why large participation can coexist with zero organizational payroll employment. [Membership and purpose](https://guildoforegonwoodworkers.org/Who-We-Are) · [Dues](https://guildoforegonwoodworkers.com/page-1863380) · [Shop access](https://www.guildoforegonwoodworkers.org/About-the-Guild)

## What to measure across these cases

Collect production, income, and participation separately. A project ledger should identify work performed, output, customer, labor hours, expenses, and payment. A facility ledger should identify access, use, and availability. A person can appear in both and in multiple directories. The public accounts establish different ways of producing; their financial results and the prevalence of those arrangements need community evidence.
