# Fire in Oregon: project charter

This is the working charter for the public guide and its first landscape investigation. Updated September 30, 2026.

## The question

What would it take to carry out and maintain the documented priorities in one Oregon landscape, what would that work protect or improve, and how would we know whether it helped?

The guide explains how fire works and how people choose among fire, mechanical work, home protection, preparedness, and response. The landscape investigation follows actual decisions from plan to funding, staffing, work, maintenance, and observed results. Each new feature or dataset must help answer a reader question or resolve a named uncertainty.

## The deliverables

1. A continuous, illustrated guide at `/oregon-fire`, readable without a map or video.
2. A documented pilot landscape with a public dossier, activity and funding timelines, evidence links, missing-data register, and versioned export.
3. A short decision brief for residents, practitioners, and funders stating what is known, what remains, and who can act.

Edan leads editorial decisions and relationships. AI assists with acquisition, correspondence preparation, extraction, reconciliation, analysis, implementation, and verification. Outside experts review specific claims when they are willing; review does not imply endorsement.

## Editorial rules

- Separate recorded expenditure, budget or award, proposed future cost, modeled benefit, observed outcome, and interpretation.
- Keep project, treatment-unit, wildfire-perimeter, and measured-effect geographies distinct. Preserve original source IDs, date precision, acreage basis, and repeated work.
- Report smoke, community burdens, ecological effects, and uncertainty alongside expected benefits.
- Do not turn acres treated into a resilience score or apply a study-wide return on investment to an individual Oregon project.
- Private interviews are leads for questions, not public evidence. Publish attributed words or distinctive anecdotes only with specific permission. Use public documents for independently sourced explanations. Keep identified cultural-fire records under the relevant program's review process.

## First landscape decision

Compare Ashland/Rogue Valley, Central Oregon/Deschutes, and Wallowa/northeastern Oregon. Score documented finance (30), linked activities and geography (25), responsive custodian (20), monitoring (15), and community relevance (10). A candidate qualifies only with a verifiable project boundary, at least one completed activity with attributable expenditure records, and a custodian able to clarify those records. Select the highest-scoring qualified candidate. If none qualifies, publish guide improvements while requests continue.

For the selected landscape, reconstruct three to five treatment sequences where records permit. Ask what was planned, funded, prepared, completed, monitored, and scheduled for maintenance. Distinguish documented delivery constraints from inferred ones.

## Research and release loop

Read existing records and mail threads; identify the smallest missing item; ask the verified custodian for one or two existing records in writing; preserve the response privately; extract claims with exact locators; reconcile; seek a focused correction or expert review; publish only approved evidence. Track each question, source, request, and change in the registers linked from the README. Never treat silence as confirmation.

Ship verified explanations before every requested record arrives. A public scenario requires locally supportable inputs and qualified review; otherwise show documented costs and the specific missing inputs. Keep the prior published data version available for rollback.

## First 90 days

Weeks 1–2: establish the registers, compare candidates, and request bounded records. Weeks 3–4: revise the public guide and costs explanation. Weeks 5–8: build the pilot's first reconciled expenditure-to-work chain. Weeks 9–12: release the reviewed landscape dossier, versioned evidence, and short decision brief. These are work stages, not guaranteed agency response dates.

Success means a newcomer can explain why a place was selected, what work happened, what it cost, why it may need repeating, what effects were observed, and what remains unknown. A practitioner can trace and correct each important claim.
