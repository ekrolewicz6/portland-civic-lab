The short version

Everything below was checked on September 10, 2026. "Queried" means the endpoint was hit and its schema and row counts read.

  1. Prescribed fire on state, private, and most federal forestland in Oregon is registered in one system: ODF's Smoke Data System. Federal units register there too (the 2025 annual report lists Deschutes NF, Mt. Hood NF, BLM districts and Crater Lake NP by name). The record includes latitude and longitude, acres, burn type, a burn reason code, fuel loading, and, after the burn, acres and tons burned, ignition time, fuel moistures, days since rain, and wind. In 2025: 2,946 registered units, 2,717 burned, 179,230 acres. The system needs a login and the public map shows only a rolling one-day and three-day window. An export of the registration and accomplishment tables for all years is the single most valuable request. That email goes first.
  2. Two federal datasets are fully public and go back decades. BLM Oregon/Washington prescribed fire polygons (36,966 completed burns with a REASON field and a NEPA plan ID) and Forest Service FACTS fuel treatments (20,531 Oregon fire-treatment records with NEPA document names and a purpose code). They can be pulled today.
  3. Wildfire history is well covered by public feeds. ODF fire occurrence points 1960 to 2024 (69,744 fires with cause and timing); NIFC/IRWIN incident locations and perimeters for all agencies, refreshed every five minutes, with prescribed fire as its own category; NWCC large-fire perimeters since 2000; Oregon Explorer's cleaned perimeter history through 2025; MTBS severity for fires over 1,000 acres.
  4. The "why this location" answer lives in three places: the NEPA documents behind federal burns (linked by ID from FACTS and BLM records), the burn reason code and comments in ODF's system, and landscape priority layers (ODF's 20-Year Strategy priority geographies, county CWPPs, Forest Service Wildfire Crisis Strategy landscapes). The plain-language version comes from practitioners, which is what the certified burn manager directory and the burn associations are for.
  5. Two things exist only as records requests: ODA's Willamette Valley field burning program (up to 15,000 acres a year, registered by April 1) and anything historical from ODF's system. Backyard and debris burn permits come from local fire districts and are not centralized; leave them out of version one.

Tribal, NGO and landowner burns

These register with ODF when on forestland, so they should appear in an export, but the reasons and the cultural context will not.

  • Prescribed Fire Liability Program enrollment map (HB 4016, 2024): enrolled prescribed and cultural burns since 2024. link only
  • Certified Burn Manager directory: 23 people as of August 2026, in the table below and in cbm-directory.csv.
  • Prescribed burn associations (seven as of January 2026): Rogue Valley, Umpqua, Mt. Adams, Central Oregon Prescribed Burn Co-op, North Valley, Northeast Oregon, Southern Blues. OSU Extension keeps the list (the page refused automated access; open it in a browser).
  • The Nature Conservancy Oregon: Sycan Marsh Preserve (30,000 acres, Klamath Basin) is the state's most studied prescribed fire site. TNC's fire program director is in the CBM directory.
  • Klamath Tribes Wildland Fire Program: spring 2026 burns and a 2025 program report.
  • Grand Ronde, Siletz and Warm Springs are partners in a 2024 NFWF-funded ecocultural burning project. Warm Springs traditional fire use is documented in a USFS research paper.
  • Lomakatsi Restoration Project and the Ashland Forest Resiliency partnership (City of Ashland, Lomakatsi, Rogue River-Siskiyou NF, TNC) run the Rogue Basin TREX.
  • Willamette Ignitions Network (Eugene) trains burn crews and hosts the September 14–17, 2026 CBM course.

Agricultural field burning (ODA)

Willamette Valley grass seed and cereal residue, up to 15,000 acres a year in the north valley, run by ODA with ODF meteorologists making the daily calls. Every field is registered by April 1. No public dataset was found. request Field registrations and daily accomplishments by year. Program line 503-986-4701 (older material) or 503-986-4550 (current page), info@oda.oregon.gov. Rules: OAR 603-077.

Backyard, debris and open burning

Permits come from local fire districts (many use beforeyouburn.com), with air rules from DEQ statewide and LRAPA in Lane County. There is no statewide registry. Leave out of version one; revisit with a records request to a few large districts if the map needs it.

Context layers

Tier 3. What makes a burn or a fire legible: smoke, weather, hazard, priorities, evacuation, utilities.

Smoke and air quality
DEQ AQI and hourly monitoring at aqi.oregon.gov (history by records request or the EPA AQS API); AirNow fire and smoke map; PurpleAir API; ODF smoke forecasts and the intrusion tables in the annual reports; the interagency blog at oregonsmoke.org. The smoke sensitive receptor area layer is public.
Fire weather and danger
ODF's live Fire Danger Level, Industrial Fire Precaution Level, Regulated Use Area and Significant Fire Potential layers (Hosted folder on gis.odf.oregon.gov/odfags); NWS fire weather zones (in the ODF web map); Red Flag warnings via the NWS API; RAWS stations via Synoptic; NWCC Predictive Services outlooks.
Hazard and risk
The 2025 Statewide Wildfire Hazard Map was repealed as a regulatory instrument by SB 83 (2025) but the data stands; the Oregon Wildfire Risk Explorer is being rebuilt (expected early 2026; check status); Wildfire Risk to Communities (USFS); LANDFIRE fuels and fire regimes.
Why-here layers
ODF 20-Year Strategy Priority Geographies (feature service on the ODF hub); county CWPPs via the Oregon CWPP Planning Tool; Forest Service Wildfire Crisis Strategy landscapes; ODF Landscape Resiliency Program projects (2023–25 implementation summary).
Evacuation and response
Genasys Protect zones (all 36 counties and nine tribes under a state contract); Public RAPTOR; the OEM ArcGIS hub.
Boundaries
ODF forest protection districts, ownership and land management, wilderness, fire weather zones, PLSS. All on the ODF hub.
Utilities
PUC wildfire mitigation plans (UM 2207 Pacific Power, UM 2208 PGE, UM 2209 Idaho Power; rules in OAR 860-300 under AR 638; 2026–2028 plans approved June 2026). Plans contain the utilities' high fire risk zones and ignition histories; PSPS lessons-learned reports are due each December 31. No structured ignition dataset is published; ask PUC safety staff.
Treatment effectiveness
FTEM records every time a wildfire meets a prior treatment. It lives in IFTDSS behind a NIFC login; public dashboard at fireportal.usda.gov/ftem. Worth a request once the treatment layers are in, because it answers "did the burn work."
Policy timeline for captions
SB 762 (2021) created the Certified Burn Manager program, the Landscape Resiliency Program and the hazard map; SB 80 (2023) revised them; HB 4016 (2024) created the Prescribed Fire Liability pilot; SB 83 (2025) repealed the hazard map and its mandates. The Wildfire Programs Advisory Council reports each October (2025 report).

Who does what

What each group actually does, so the asks land with the right people.

ODF Smoke Management Program (Salem)
Runs the statewide registry for prescribed burning on forestland. Its meteorologists issue the daily burn instructions that decide whether registered units may ignite. Its staff maintain the Smoke Data System, the daily and weekly PDFs, the public map and the annual report, and run the Certified Burn Manager program and the Prescribed Fire Liability pilot. This is the office that holds the per-burn history.
ODF district offices
Twelve protection districts do the field work: take registrations from landowners, inspect units, relay burn instructions, enter accomplishments. If Salem says the history is held locally, these are the next stop.
Forest Service ranger districts (fuels staff)
Plan and run burns on national forests: write the burn plans, carry the NEPA decision, register with ODF, post planned units to the Region 6 tracker. Each unit in the tracker lists its district phone. The Region 6 office in Portland coordinates and publishes the annual fire summaries.
BLM district fuels programs
Same role on BLM land. The OR/WA state office publishes the prescribed fire polygon dataset and the season's planned burns.
Certified Burn Managers
People ODF has certified (24 hours of coursework, three training burns, five-year certification) to plan and lead prescribed burns on ODF-protected land. When a landowner uses a CBM and follows the burn plan, both are protected from civil liability (SB 762; OAR 629-042-1000 to 1070). A CBM writes the burn plan (objectives, weather prescription, ignition pattern, holding resources, contingencies), files the smoke registration with the ODF district, waits for the daily instruction, runs the burn as burn boss, and files the accomplishment. They know why each unit was chosen, and they hold the plans and the photos. Directory columns: Insured means they carry their own liability insurance; For hire means they will burn for other landowners; Willing to mentor means they take trainees on burns, which is how new CBMs get their three training fires.
Prescribed burn associations
Landowner cooperatives that pool equipment, people and know-how to burn each other's land, usually with an OSU Extension fire specialist helping. Seven exist as of January 2026. They are the route to private-land burns and to the landowners' own reasons.
OSU Extension Fire Program
Regional fire specialists who help PBAs form, teach the CBM course and publish the guides. Neutral, well connected, and used to explaining fire to the public; a natural advisor.
OSU Institute for Natural Resources / Oregon Explorer
Built the Wildfire Risk Explorer, the CWPP planning tool and the cleaned statewide perimeter history. They have already solved the cleaning problems we will hit.
The Nature Conservancy Oregon
Runs prescribed fire at Sycan Marsh and elsewhere, hosts research, and employs two of the 23 CBMs including its fire program director. The most experienced non-agency burn program in the state.
Tribal fire programs
The Klamath Tribes Wildland Fire Program burns on tribal and adjacent federal land. Warm Springs, Grand Ronde and Siletz are restoring ecocultural burning. Approach as partners whose burns may or may not belong on a public map, on their terms.
NWCC (Portland)
The Northwest Geographic Area Coordination Center. Compiles large fire perimeters and daily situation reports for Oregon and Washington; runs Predictive Services.
DEQ Air Quality
Co-administers the smoke management plan, designates smoke sensitive receptor areas with ODF, runs the monitoring network. Holds the monitor history.
ODA Smoke Management Program
Registers and permits Willamette Valley field burning and makes daily calls with ODF meteorologists. Holds field-level registrations and accomplishments that are not published.
Oregon PUC Safety Division
Reviews the three investor-owned utilities' wildfire mitigation plans and PSPS reports. The route to utility ignition and risk-zone data.
Oregon Department of Emergency Management
RAPTOR, the OEM hub, and (through ODHS's contract) Genasys evacuation zones for all counties and nine tribes.
Oregon Hazards Lab (UO)
Operates the Oregon part of the ALERTWest camera network.

Should we email all 23 burn managers?

Not yet, and not with a general "would you like to take part." The directory exists so landowners can hire them, and a cold mass email with no concrete ask will get few replies. Two steps work better:

  1. Now: six or seven targeted notes to the people who sit at hubs and would each open a door to many burns: Stacy McCarter (the data), Carrie Berger (Extension and the PBAs), Kai Sauerbrey (TNC and Sycan), John Punches or Micah Schmidt (Northeast Oregon), Richard Pasquale (Central Oregon co-op), Brent Ross (Willamette Valley), and one contractor such as Daniel Cleveland. Ask each for a 20-minute call. Draft 3 below.
  2. Later, with a prototype: one note to the whole directory and the Prescribed Fire Council list, inviting practitioners to check how their burns appear and add the reason for each unit in their own words. That is a specific ask that respects their time and gets us the narrative layer. Draft 9.

The tribal programs are a separate conversation, in person if possible, before anything of theirs appears on the map.

On the records route. Oregon's Public Records Law (ORS 192.311 to 192.478) covers ODF, ODA, DEQ, PUC and OEM. Agencies may charge fees but can reduce or waive them when release serves the public interest (ORS 192.324(5)). ODF's portal gives the first 30 minutes of staff time free.
