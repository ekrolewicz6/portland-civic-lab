Since 2019 Portland Public Schools has lost one student in eight, by its own count [budget-fy2026-27-supt-message], taken in almost $115 million of one-time federal relief, gone through the first teacher strike in its history, cut budgets several years running, eliminated hundreds of jobs, and begun planning school closures on a scale the city has not seen in decades. In the same stretch its headline budget grew from $1.4 billion to $2.77 billion. (Each figure is sourced where it appears below; the few that exist only in press reporting are labeled as such.)

Every one of those facts is true. This document explains how they fit together, what actually decides where the money goes, where money is demonstrably doing less than it could, and which questions the public record cannot answer.

Two questions organize everything that follows.

**First: when a dollar arrives at PPS, what decides whether it reaches a student, who set that rule, and who could change it?** Most of what Portlanders argue about is settled by rules nobody in the school board room controls. A state formula written in 1991 makes Portland's property wealth mostly irrelevant to Portland's classrooms. A pair of 1990s ballot measures quietly takes an eight-figure sum from the one levy voters passed specifically for teachers, and the leak is growing fast. Bond dollars legally cannot buy a single hour of instruction. And a quirk of Oregon budget law means the seven board members review their own budget with no citizen sitting beside them.

**Second: of the dollars PPS actually controls, which are demonstrably doing less for students than they could, by whose standard, and what would it take to know?** That is the honest version of "where is the waste." It has real answers. Some of them are large. And the largest one is not a line item at all. It is information: a $2.77 billion organization (section 03) that publishes no financial statements between July and the following January, discovered a mid-year hole it should have seen coming, and gives its only citizen reviewers nine working days.

One device runs through the whole document. Every dollar gets one of four tags:

- **Locked.** Legally cannot move. Bond proceeds, bond debt service, restricted state and federal grants. A wasted locked dollar is real waste, but it was never teacher money, and any claim that treats it as teacher money is wrong on arrival.
- **Committed.** Legally movable but pinned by contracts and mandates: collective bargaining agreements, pension rates, special education obligations, keeping the lights on.
- **Movable.** The slice where board choices actually bite.
- **Unknown.** The public record cannot tell. Section 13 treats that as a finding, not a footnote.

No PPS dollar figure appears in this document without knowing which tag it wears.

**The three findings that survive every caveat:**

1. **The empty chair.** Oregon law gives nearly every school district a budget committee that is half appointed citizens. A carve-out for large districts in counties with a tax supervising commission, ORS 294.423, lets the PPS board serve as its own budget committee, and it does. The substitute is a volunteer advisory committee that got nine working days this year to review $2.8 billion and wrote, plainly, that oversight on that timeline "risks becoming symbolic rather than substantive" [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p2].
2. **The levy leak.** Voters renewed a $1.99 teacher levy in 2024. The tax code delivers about $1.51 of it per thousand dollars of value; compression losses have grown 72 percent in three years and are projected to exceed $53M next year [tscc-annual-report-2024-25-general p50; budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p28]. Fewer teachers per levy dollar is not a management failure. It is the 1990s tax constitution working exactly as written, and only Salem can fix it.
3. **The forecast problem** *(corrected August 30, 2026; see the log above)*. PPS does publish interim reports, quarterly since FY2023-24 and monthly for FY2025-26 [pps-q1-fy2025-26 through pps-p11-fy2025-26]. What they show is the finding: the district's own forecast of its year-end General Fund balance swung from $41.3M (September) to $17.7M (December) to $26.8M (March) to $34.6M (May) within one fiscal year, while the mid-year scramble happened anyway. The books exist; nothing forces a reconciliation when they whipsaw. And the artifacts the 2019 state audit asked for, a readable summary and multi-year trend data, still do not exist in the FY2026-27 book [sos-audit-2019-01; sos-secretary-of-state-sos-recommendation-follow-up-report; see section 10].

**The annual question**, for every April, for any board member or parent: *Does the next dollar reach a student, and can you show me?*

# 02 · What we read

This analysis rests on the public paper trail, all of it we could find.

The district posts twenty-one consecutive annual budget books, FY2006-07 through FY2026-27; we fetched and read the eleven inside this document's horizon, FY2016-17 forward, and registered and archived the rest. It posts audited Annual Comprehensive Financial Reports back to fiscal 2002; we worked through FY2016 to FY2025. The budget reviews of the Tax Supervising and Conservation Commission, the Governor-appointed body that must certify every Multnomah County budget before adoption, plus its all-district annual reports. Five years of reports from the Community Budget Review Committee, the district's volunteer citizen reviewers, and four of their annual reviews of the teachers levy. Eleven independent bond audits spanning the 2012, 2017, and 2020 construction bonds, plus ten of the auditors' implementation-status trackers. The 98-page Oregon Secretary of State audit of January 2019 and its 2022 follow-up. Six current union contracts. The Portland State University enrollment forecasts as reproduced in the TSCC reviews and the district's own books (the university repository blocks automated retrieval of the originals; direct copies are queued for manual pickup). State School Fund allocation runs from the Oregon Department of Education. In total: 120 documents fetched and checksummed of 199 registered, and a dataset of more than 5,400 extracted figures, each carrying the document and page it came from.

Three warnings about this terrain, learned the hard way.

First, **the record is rotting.** PPS documents live behind opaque content-manager links that have already broken once during a website migration. The state's own copy of its 2019 audit of PPS vanished from sos.oregon.gov sometime this spring; we recovered the full report from the copy PPS itself hosts. Everything we cite is archived.

Second, **the official numbers disagree with each other more than you would hope.** Our extraction logged nineteen cross-document contradictions in TSCC's own tables, an audited ACFR that prints an enrollment figure off by roughly 7,800 students for 2022-23, a CBRC report that states the budget gap as both $50 million and $56.3 million, and consecutive official levy reviews that report 851 and 922 teachers funded in the same year. None of these change the story's direction. All of them say something about the state of financial quality control, and section 10 returns to that.

Third, **the internet will lie to you about this district.** Portland, Maine's school district dominates search results for "Portland Public Schools budget," down to a plausible-looking voter-approved budget figure. Oregon school budgets never go to a voter referendum. Every figure here is domain-verified to the Oregon documents.

What does *not* exist in public is as instructive as what does, and section 13 catalogs it: no bond disclosures on any PPS website, no published cost model for the strike settlement, no index of mid-year budget changes, no state performance audit since 2019, and, in the interim reports that do exist, no forecast-accuracy standard that forces action when the numbers whipsaw.

# 03 · Four kinds of money

*Or: why "$2.77 billion" and "brutal cuts" are both true.*

Start with the number everyone quotes. The adopted FY2026-27 budget is $2,768,590,878 [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p317]. Last year's was $2.035 billion [budget-fy2025-26-vol1 p89]. So the budget grew about $733 million while the district cut 322 jobs. That looks insane. It is actually just fund accounting.

A school district budget is several separate pots of money that are illegal to mix. Here is the whole jump, pot by pot:

| Fund type | FY2025-26 | FY2026-27 | Change | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| General Fund | $868.6M | $862.1M | **−$6.5M** | movable+committed |
| Special Revenue | $224.8M | $223.9M | −$0.9M | mostly locked |
| Debt Service | $272.7M | $278.9M | +$6.2M | locked |
| Capital Projects | $643.8M | $1,382.5M | **+$738.7M** | locked |
| Internal Service | $25.3M | $21.2M | −$4.1M | committed |

Sources: [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p108, p122, p157, p173, p205; budget-fy2025-26-vol1 p89-200].

The entire growth, and then some, is the Capital Projects line. In February 2026 the district sold $660 million of bonds, $90 million remaining under the 2020 authorization and $570 million under the $1.83 billion bond voters approved in May 2025 [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p230]. That money exists to rebuild Jefferson, Cleveland, and Ida B. Wells high schools and to fund repairs, curriculum, and technology. Spending it on teachers would be illegal. Meanwhile the General Fund, the pot that pays for teaching, **fell** by $6.5 million, the first year-over-year decline in the eleven years we assembled [derived: gf_series].

So when the district says it is cutting and a critic says the budget has never been bigger, both are pointing at real numbers. The problem is that no PPS public document lays the two side by side in plain language. The Community Budget Review Committee made exactly this complaint about last year's budget optics, noting the distinction was "not clearly communicated in public-facing documents" [cbrc-2025-26-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p12]. The 2019 state audit asked for a readable budget summary with trends. Seven years later, the clearest one-page decomposition of the PPS budget may be the table above.

Two more numbers calibrate everything downstream.

**About 30 percent of the "budget" is money the district already had.** The FY2025-26 budget counted $608.5M of beginning fund balance as a resource; 84.6 percent of that was unspent bond money sitting in the capital fund [tscc-review-fy2026 p18, p6]. A budget total is resources, not spending.

**The per-student arithmetic depends entirely on the denominator you choose.** The CBRC put it sharply this year: Volume 2 of the budget shows about $11,100 per student arriving at schools directly, while Volume 1 implies roughly $26,300 per student once construction and debt are set aside, "more than twice as much" [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p3]. Both are computed from real numbers. Neither is dishonest. The gap between them is central administration, districtwide programs, transportation, food service, and everything else that is real spending but not a teacher in a room. Any argument that quotes one of these numbers without the other is selling something.

The rule this document keeps: **no dollar figure without its ledger tag.** It is the single discipline that keeps the rest of the analysis honest.

# 04 · Where the money comes from: Salem's machine

The strangest fact in Oregon school finance is this: **for operating money, Portland's property wealth mostly buys Portland's schools nothing.**

Since 1991, Oregon has run school funding through an equalization formula. The state sets a per-student funding target, weights each student for extra need, and then fills the gap between a district's local property tax revenue and its formula total. The two identities that govern everything [lro-5-24-equalization p6-7]:

> District formula revenue = State School Fund grant + local revenue
> State School Fund grant = formula revenue − local revenue

Read the second line again. Every dollar of ordinary property tax PPS collects is subtracted from its state check. If Portland's tax base booms, the state check shrinks by the same amount. If it craters, the state fills the hole. The Legislative Revenue Office states it flatly: if local revenues are high, state aid is low; if local revenues are low, state aid is high [lro-5-24-equalization p6]. The statute even counts "uncertified tax capacity," tax room a district declines to levy, as local revenue against the state grant, and the formula overrides the nominal distribution rules of the Common School Fund and several other streams [ORS 327.011; lro-5-24-equalization p7]. (The uncertified-capacity reading is ours from the statute's text; the LRO report does not discuss it.)

The weights are where policy lives. Each student counts as 1.0 in average daily membership; a student with a disability adds 1.0 more, but the state only pays that weight up to **11 percent of enrollment**, a cap set in the 1990s. PPS reports about 17 percent of its students receive special education services [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p11]. The gap between the capped weight and the actual cost lands on the General Fund, and federal maintenance-of-effort rules rightly prevent cutting it. An English language learner adds 0.5; students in poverty, foster care, and several other categories add 0.25 (weights per ORS 327.013; the LRO report's weights table did not survive into our text extraction, so the statute is the citable source). A statewide high-cost disability pool, capped at $55 million a year and prorated, covers a fraction of costs above $30,000 per student [lro-5-24-equalization p9-11]. One mercy for a shrinking district: the formula pays on the *higher* of this year's or last year's weighted count, so enrollment loss hits revenue with a one-year lag instead of instantly.

For FY2026-27 the mechanics produce: a State School Fund line of $310.3 million in the adopted book [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p95], against a statewide appropriation of $11.36 billion for the biennium [tscc-review-fy2026 p8]. Property taxes for operations, the permanent rate plus the gap tax, produce about $360 million [tscc-review-fy2026 p18], most of which the formula silently nets against the state grant. Weighted enrollment for funding purposes: 51,390 [budget-fy2026-27-supt-message].

What actually adds money, because it sits **outside** the formula:

| Stream | FY26-27 order of magnitude | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Local option levy | ~$104M budgeted collections | capped by statute and compressed by Measure 5; section 05 |
| GO bond levies | $184.25M levied for debt | locked to debt service [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p224, p318] |
| Student Success Act (SIA) | $43.7M rising to $45.5M | corporate activity tax; poverty weights doubled [ode-sia-allocation-25-27 p9] |
| High School Success (M98) | $12.6M rising to $13.1M | 9-12 only; got its own fund only in FY2025-26 [ode-hss-allocation-25-27; budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p146] |
| Federal grants (Title I, IDEA, nutrition) | inside $223.9M special revenue | non-supplantable |
| City Arts Tax | $5.3M | the city's tax, PPS's arts teachers [tscc-review-fy2026 p13] |
| Gifts, fees, construction excise | small | excluded "to balance equalization with local control" |

And the one-time stream that shaped this whole era: ESSER, almost $115 million of federal pandemic relief awarded to the district and its partners across three tranches [budget-fy2023-24-vol1], fully outside the formula, fully expired as of September 2024. Section 07 shows what the district did with it and what happened when it stopped.

The political consequence of this architecture is easy to miss and worth saying plainly. When Portlanders vote themselves higher ordinary school taxes, they are, for operations, mostly voting to reduce the state's bill. The only local levers that genuinely add operating money are the levy and the handful of outside streams above. Which is why the next section matters more than its size suggests.

# 05 · The two taxes that do help, and the leak in one of them

PPS levies four property tax lines. On a Portland tax bill for FY2026-27 they are: the permanent rate, $4.7743 per $1,000 of assessed value; the gap tax, $0.5038; the local option levy, $1.9900; and the bond levy, about $2.5046 [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p226]. The first two are formula revenue, mostly offsetting the state check. The last two are the ones that add.

**The bond levy** is straightforward: it is excluded from the constitutional caps entirely, pays only debt, and voters have approved four of them since 2012, at 66 percent in 2012, 66 percent in 2017 [tscc-measure-26-215-review p2], approximately 75 percent in 2020 (press-reported; the corpus election table stops at 2017), and in May 2025 the $1.83 billion measure. (They said no once, in 2011, by 668 votes [tscc-measure-26-215-review p2].) The district has held the combined bond rate near a self-imposed target, and total debt now stands at $2.13 billion [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p232], comfortably inside the statutory ceiling of 7.95 percent of real market value, of which PPS uses about 11 percent of capacity [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p231]. Whether the bond *programs* deliver what was promised is section 10's business.

**The local option levy** is where the leak is. It exists because of the interaction of two 1990s constitutional amendments. Measure 5 (1990) caps education taxes at $5.00 per $1,000 of a property's *real market value*. Measure 50 (1997) moved the tax base to a separate, lower *assessed value* that can grow only 3 percent a year. The levy lives in the space between those two systems, and when a property's taxes hit the Measure 5 ceiling, the shortfall, called compression, is taken **from the local option levy first**, down to zero, before any other line is touched.

Here is what that has done. The figures are the total Measure 5 loss across all PPS tax lines; TSCC's analysis shows most of it, roughly 80 to 88 percent depending on the year, lands on the local option levy [tscc-review-fy2024 p3, p6; tscc-review-fy2025 p3; tscc-annual-report-2024-25-general p50; budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p28]:

| Year | Measure 5 loss, all PPS lines |
|---|---|
| 2019-20 | $20.9M |
| 2020-21 | $23.0M |
| 2021-22 | $24.8M |
| 2022-23 | $25.2M |
| 2023-24 | $35.7M |
| 2024-25 | $42.6M |
| 2026-27 (projected) | $53.4M |

A 72 percent jump in three years, and still climbing. In FY2024-25 the levy alone was extended at $143.1 million and lost $34.2 million of the $42.6 million total to compression, an effective rate of **$1.5142** against the $1.99 voters approved [tscc-annual-report-2024-25-general p50]. TSCC's comparative tables show PPS is the largest local-option compression loser in Multnomah County. The mechanism is perverse: compression spikes when real market values stagnate while assessed values keep ratcheting up 3 percent, which is exactly Portland's post-2022 market. The FY2026-27 book projects the levy will be *imposed* at $149.9 million and *collected* at $103.9 million [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p224, p108].

Now put people to it. The levy is legally restricted to teacher salaries and benefits, held in a General Fund sub-account, and audited for that every year, cleanly [cbrc-2024-25-cbrc-local-option-levy-review p2]. The CBRC's levy reviews track what flat receipts and rising costs do:

| 2023-24 | $108.8M | ~802 | $135,739 |

The committee's own conclusion: "Without intervention, PPS could lose nearly one-quarter of its Levy-funded teaching positions by 2025-26 compared to 2019-20" [cbrc-2024-25-cbrc-local-option-levy-review p2]. Note the campaign for the 2024 renewal promised "approximately 660" positions, a number the levy currently beats; the earlier promise for the 2019 measure was about 825. And note one wart we could not resolve: consecutive official reviews report 851 and 922 positions for the same year, 2022-23, an 8 percent discrepancy neither document explains.

The homeowner's view, for a house at $300,000 assessed value: $2,932 a year across all four PPS lines at the FY2026-27 combined rate of $9.7727 [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p226], before compression trims what the district actually receives. What the homeowner is not told anywhere on the bill is that the $1.99 line delivers about $1.51, and that the delivered amount will keep shrinking as long as Salem leaves Measure 5 arithmetic alone.

This is the sharpest example of the document's first organizing question. Levy erosion looks like district mismanagement, gets argued about as if it were district mismanagement, and is entirely a state constitutional design problem. A board member who wants more teachers per levy dollar has exactly one venue: the legislature.

# 06 · Who decides: the empty chair at the budget table

The formal calendar is set by Oregon's Local Budget Law, ORS chapter 294, and it is genuinely rigorous on paper. A budget officer prepares a proposed budget. A budget committee holds public meetings, takes comment, approves the budget, and sets the tax levies. The approved budget goes, because this is Multnomah County, to the Tax Supervising and Conservation Commission, which holds its own public hearing and must certify the budget before adoption. The board then holds a hearing, adopts, appropriates, and imposes taxes by June 30 [dor-local-budgeting-manual; ORS 294.635]. Appropriations are legal ceilings by major function; overspending one is unlawful; mid-year changes above 10 percent of a fund require a public hearing on a supplemental budget [ORS 294.456, 294.471, 294.473].

Now the part almost nobody in Portland knows. For most Oregon districts, ORS 294.414 requires the budget committee to be the elected board **plus an equal number of appointed citizen electors**, ordinary residents with the same seat at the table, the same right to question the budget officer, the same vote on approving the budget. Beaverton parents get that. Salem-Keizer parents get that.

Portland parents do not. ORS 294.423 provides that in a municipal corporation with more than 200,000 people located in a county with a tax supervising commission, the governing body itself may serve as the budget committee, and PPS's board does exactly that: "The Board, sitting as the Budget Committee, reviews and discusses the" proposed budget, in the district's own words [budget-fy2026-27-vol1]. The seven people who review the budget are the seven people who adopt it. The statutory citizen half of the table, everywhere else in Oregon, is an empty chair here.

Two bodies partially compensate, neither with power.

**TSCC** certifies compliance, and its reviews are the best independent numbers on PPS anywhere; this document leans on them constantly. But its recommendations are advisory, its certification checks legal form rather than wisdom, and its last three PPS certification letters contained no objections [tscc-review-fy2026 p18; tscc-review-fy2025 p16; tscc-review-fy2024 p17].

**The CBRC** is the district's own creation: volunteers, appointed by the board, reviewing the superintendent's proposal and monitoring the levy. Their reports are sharp, specific, and increasingly blunt about their own position. This year: nine working days to evaluate $2.8 billion, a timeline the committee called "inadequate for the responsibility entrusted to this body," with the warning that oversight on such terms "risks becoming symbolic rather than substantive" [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p2]. The year before: "In our last report, we noted the need for more time. That recommendation remains unaddressed" [cbrc-2025-26-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p12]. The committee has documented the nine-day squeeze and asked for more time three years running, after a related plea for deeper work sessions the year before that. Section 10 tallies what else it has asked for repeatedly and not received.

Inside the building, the budget officer in practice is the superintendent, Dr. Kimberlee Armstrong, in the role since July 2024, the district's third leader within that single year after Guadalupe Guerrero's mid-year departure and Sandy Husk's interim term [acfr-fy2024; budget-fy2026-27-vol1]. The board seated four new members in July 2025, a majority turnover in one election (press-reported). The adopted FY2026-27 budget passed on June 23, 2026 [budget-fy2026-27-calendar-amended]; the session details reported in the press, a roughly six-hour meeting, one abstention, a pause on new artificial intelligence contracts, a freeze on discretionary travel, and a failed amendment to restore $13.1 million of laid-off staff, are carried here as press reporting [news-opb-2026-06-25-adoption, press].

The values framework the board budgets under, in its own documents: a Racial Educational Equity Policy adopted unanimously in 2011, expressed in the budget through an equity staffing formula (section 11 covers the federal lawsuit now aimed at it); a 2019 reserve policy targeting 5 to 10 percent of General Fund resources with a stated goal of 10, currently pinned at the 5 percent floor [tscc-review-fy2026 p10]; and, since 2025, a move toward "student outcomes focused governance," a model that deliberately narrows board attention away from operational detail. Reasonable people can defend that model. Its timing is awkward: it reduces line-item scrutiny in the exact years when closures, layoffs, and a structural deficit most demand it, in a district where the board is also, by statute, the only budget committee there is.

TSCC forecasts after 2024-25; the TSCC chart is a slightly different series from the ACFR headcount, so treat cross-series comparisons as approximate.*

Three shapes jump out of that table. Nominal operating money rose every single year until this one. In purchasing power, the General Fund peaked in 2021-22 at $951 million and has since lost about 9 percent. And enrollment fell off a cliff whose edge was 2019-20: essentially flat near 49,500 for four years, then down roughly 8,100 students, 16 percent, in seven.

The narrative, pinned to those numbers:

**2016-2019: the plateau.** Enrollment flat, budgets growing with the economy, the 2012 bond program finishing over budget but delivering (section 10), the 2017 bond ramping up. In January 2019 the Secretary of State published a 98-page joint audit of PPS and the state education department with 26 recommendations, faulting, among much else, budget documents with limited program detail, no benchmarking, and little visibility into staffing and spending changes over time [sos-audit-2019-01]. The district accepted all recommendations.

**2019-2021: the shock and the sugar.** The Student Success Act's new corporate tax money arrived. Then COVID. Kindergarten families in particular did not come back; the district's capture rate of eligible kindergartners fell from roughly 80 percent to about 70 percent and stayed there (a PSU-forecast figure carried in press reporting; the underlying forecast documents are queued for manual pickup). Enrollment dropped 2,164 students in 2020-21 alone. But dollars went the other way: almost $115 million of federal ESSER relief was awarded to the district and its partners across three tranches [budget-fy2023-24-vol1], and the state check, cushioned by the formula's prior-year enrollment provision, kept growing. Real General Fund purchasing power hit its all-time peak in 2021-22, immediately after the two heaviest years of enrollment loss.

**The braid.** The district spent its ESSER heavily on people and programs: learning acceleration specialists, mental health supports, summer programming, deliberately braided with Student Investment Account money. Federal guidance encouraged exactly this. But it meant one-time money was carrying *ongoing* commitments, and the district's own citizen reviewers saw it in real time. The CBRC, spring 2023: "CBRC is concerned that these additional funding sources are hiding the looming shortfall due to ongoing enrollment decline from the general public" [cbrc-2023-24-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p3]. Going into the final year, the district still budgeted $36.3 million of remaining ESSER for FY2023-24, against an obligation deadline of September 2024 [tscc-review-fy2024 p7].

**November 2023: the strike.** The first in district history. The duration (nearly four instructional weeks), the settlement's cumulative 13.8 percent cost-of-living increase, and the district's roughly $175 million three-year cost estimate are press-reported figures [news-opb-2023-11-29-strike-faq, press]; the signed contract itself is in the corpus [cba-pat-2023-2026], but no district cost model has ever been published (section 13, request R3). The CBRC recorded that it had asked, during the negotiation year, whether budgeted funds could cover the anticipated contracts, "did not receive information sufficient" to advise, and said so in writing [cbrc-2024-25-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p11].

**2023-2027: the unwind, cut by cut.** The sequence, from TSCC's reviews and the adopted books: FY2023-24, about 281 positions cut, gap patched with one-time resources and reserves. FY2024-25, a $30 million gap, half central office, half schools. FY2025-26, a $40 million gap, $17 million central, $23 million schools, about 183 licensed positions, and the PERS Rate Stabilization Reserve drained to the last $394,000, moving $24 million into the General Fund as what the district's own FAQ called a one-year solution to a multi-year challenge. Then, mid-year, the part nobody budgeted: a deficit opened inside the running FY2025-26 year, confirmed in the citizen committee's review [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p5]; the reported size, roughly $10 million, the February 2026 disclosure, and the furloughs-or-layoffs offer to unions are press reporting [news-opb-2026-02-24-midyear; news-ww-2026-02-27-furloughs, press]. FY2026-27: a $56.3 million gap [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p9; budget-book-fy2026-27-proposed-vol1], closed with 322 positions eliminated, reserves pinned at the 5 percent floor, and the total district workforce budgeted at 5,513 FTE, down 761 from FY2021-22's actual 6,274 [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p92; tscc-review-fy2026 pp1-11]. The district's own projection for FY2027-28: another deficit of more than $65 million [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p11].

Stack the named gaps alone, $30 million, $40 million, $56.3 million, and the district has cut more than $125 million out of three consecutive budgets, before counting the FY2023-24 reductions that were patched with one-time money [tscc-review-fy2025 p1; tscc-review-fy2026 p4; cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p9]. Add the *causes* and you get the honest thesis of this decade:

**A fixed-cost institution met a variable-revenue formula, with a two-year information lag, carrying commitments made on one-time money, and nobody was publishing numbers in between.** Enrollment fell 16 percent while buildings, boilers, principals, and bus routes stayed fixed. Revenue follows students out the door with a lag; costs do not follow at all without a decision, and the decisions came years after the forecasts. The strike raised the price of every remaining position. ESSER's end subtracted the money that had been papering over the arithmetic. And the mid-year surprise of 2026 happened in an institution that publishes no financial statements between budget adoption and the audit the following winter.

# 08 · Where the money goes

Composition first, then the three fights inside it.

Of the $862.1 million General Fund: 78.9 percent, $680.5 million, is salaries and benefits [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p107]; TSCC put the FY2025-26 share at 79.6 percent [tscc-review-fy2026 p15]. (A widely quoted "85 percent" figure is the statewide share of formula dollars going to compensation, not a PPS number.) By function, instruction is a bit over half of General Fund spending; by the CBRC's benchmark, PPS puts about 53 percent of its *operating* budget into instruction against a 61 percent national average, an eight-point gap the committee flagged as a preliminary finding deserving a formal study, noting also that Oregon's above-average salaries and pension costs sit inside that 53 percent, so "each instructional dollar purchases fewer teacher resources than it would in a typical district" [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p5].

**PERS.** The pension system deserves its own paragraph because it explains more of the deficit than any headline admits. PPS pays a blended contribution rate that was around 4.2 percent of eligible payroll in FY2025-26 thanks to "side accounts" the district bought by issuing pension obligation bonds in 2002, 2003, and 2021, $510.3 million of which remained outstanding at mid-2025 [acfr-fy2025 p37, p122], serviced through internal charges baked into every department's fringe rate [tscc-review-fy2026 pp10-11, 16-17]. Those side-account credits are now expiring: the CBRC describes the rate jumping "from just over 4% of each eligible employee's salary to nearly 23%" [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p10], The stabilization reserve built for exactly this moment is gone, spent in one year, just under $24 million moved to the General Fund [tscc-review-fy2026 p10]. Pension cost growth is a committed dollar: no board vote can decline it.

**Purchased services, de-confounded.** The loudest number of the spring was the CBRC's finding of "a 61.9% increase in Purchased Services over the prior year, from $269.9 million to $437.0 million," with itemized spikes: management services up $74.4 million (199 percent), legal up $10.3 million (750 percent), moving services up $2.4 million (1,452 percent), and the observation that the increase was "roughly triple the $56.3 million budget gap" [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p9]. That number is real, and it needs one large qualifier: it is an *all-funds* figure in a year when bond construction contracting ramped hard, and press reporting attributed roughly two-thirds of the total to capital funds [news-opb-2026-05-13-contracts, press]; the committee itself notes only that "a significant portion of this increase is to purchase non-classroom or building services" [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p9]. The General Fund's own purchased-services line tells a calmer story: $85.9 million in FY2019-20 to $110.7 million in FY2026-27, between 12 and a bit over 13 percent of the General Fund throughout [derived: purchased_services_gf]. The honest statement is: operational contracting has grown moderately and its composition deserves the scrutiny the CBRC demands; the eye-popping 61.9 percent headline is mostly the bond ramp wearing an operating costume, with the exact split unpublished. Section 10 takes up the specific contracts.

**Central office versus schools.** The FTE-by-function series is the cleanest evidence we have, and it cuts against the district in one specific way. From FY2021-22 actuals to the FY2025-26 adopted budget: regular instruction FTE fell from 2,467.6 to 2,166.7, a 12.2 percent cut; special programs rose 3.3 percent (as it should, given caseloads); school administration fell 7.9 percent; central business support fell 5.5 percent; and facilities acquisition staffing *grew*, bond-funded [budget-fy2025-26-vol1 p231]. Classroom staffing absorbed cuts at more than twice the percentage rate of central business support over those years. The recent budgets rebalanced somewhat: the FY2025-26 package assigned $17 million to central office against $23 million to schools [tscc-review-fy2026 p4], and the FY2026-27 book reports 142 FTE reduced from building supports and central office against 180 from schools and specialized programs [budget-fy2026-27-vol1]. The CBRC's standing complaint is not that central office is untouched; it is that no public document lets anyone verify the totals: the committee found the district's claim of central-office cuts impossible to reconcile against Function 2000 spending, which declined all of 0.5 percent in the year 65 central positions were supposedly eliminated [cbrc-2025-26-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p6]. The widely cited claim that PPS administration grew 88 percent from 2000 to 2019 while teachers grew 9 percent comes from press reporting we have not yet verified against primary documents, and this document carries it only with that label.

# 09 · Empty seats: the structure under the deficit

The deficit's deepest cause is not a contract or a contractor. It is geometry.

State revenue arrives per weighted student. Costs arrive per building, per boiler, per principal, per bus route. When 8,100 students leave and no buildings do, per-student overhead rises automatically, year after year, with no one voting for it. PPS operates on the order of 80 schools across 152 square miles for a student body that has shrunk about 16 percent from its 2017-18 peak, with TSCC's reproduction of the forecasts showing the slide continuing to 39,945 by 2028-29 [tscc-review-fy2026 p4]; longer-horizon press accounts of the PSU forecasts put the mid-2030s trough near 37,000 [news-ww-2026-08-24-forecasts, press]. Kindergarten capture, the share of eligible kindergartners who enroll, sits around 70 percent, down from about 80 before the pandemic (press-reported PSU figure); the students who never arrive in kindergarten never arrive in any later grade either. This is a birth-rate and family-migration story more than a school-quality story, and it is not reversing on any forecast the district cites.

The district's response, begun in fall 2026, is a consolidation process it calls rightsizing; the superintendent's budget message commits to "plan for consolidation and closure" [budget-fy2026-27-supt-message], and the reported scale, initially five to ten schools and by late August 2026 up to twenty, with recommendations due at the end of 2026 for effect in 2027-28, is press reporting [news-ww-2026-08-26-20-schools, press].

Hold two things at once here, because section 10 will need both.

First: **small schools are a purchase, not a waste.** A community that keeps a 250-student neighborhood elementary open is buying something real: walkability, belonging, a building that anchors a neighborhood, and closures in this district's history have landed hardest on Black and brown North and Northeast Portland, which remembers. The district is allowed to buy that. Voters are allowed to demand it.

Second: **the purchase has a price, and nobody has been publishing it.** Every year of delay between the forecasts (public and unambiguous since at least 2021-22) and the decision (beginning 2026-27, effective 2027-28 at the earliest) is a year of paying full fixed costs on seats without students in them, out of the same General Fund that cut 322 positions. What a closure actually saves is contested and school-specific, on the order of a principal, office and custodial staffing, and utilities per building, minus transition costs, and the district's own savings model is not public (section 13, request R5). What is not contested is that the district ran five consecutive years of cuts before putting the question formally on the table.

The CBRC saw the shape early and said it plainly in 2025: "The district cannot afford to make temporary programs permanent without permanent funding" [cbrc-2025-26-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p10]. The same logic applies to buildings.

# 10 · The waste examination

The user's question, everyone's question: where is the waste? Here is the standard this document applies, stated before any claim so the claims cannot bend it:

> A dollar is wasted when three things are true at once: PPS controlled it, or controlled the decision that committed it; another available use of the same dollar, in the same ledger, would predictably have done more for students; and the district had, or refused to gather, the information to know that at the time. Anything failing the first test is Salem's problem or the tax code's problem. Anything failing the second is a values disagreement. Anything failing the third is a forecasting miss, which is a process finding, not waste.

Verdicts come in five kinds: **Shown** (documented, quantified, PPS-controllable), **Likely, pending one document** (the missing document is named and requested in section 13), **A cost of delay** (a structural cost priced by years of known inaction), **A choice, not waste** (a values allocation, argued in section 11), and **Not waste** (declined, with the reason).

And one rule above all: **no total.** This document never sums a "PPS wastes $X a year" headline. The claims below live in different ledgers with different levels of certainty, and a sum would be false precision engineered for a headline. Anyone who gives you a single number for PPS waste is doing that.

## W1 · The bond overruns (locked ledger)

The record here is unusually good, because PPS, to its genuine credit, has funded an independent bond performance audit nearly every year since 2014. Those audits show, across three bond programs [per-year worksheet: bonds; bond audit exhibits as cited]:

- **2012 program ($482M authorized):** final cost at completion about $594M, delivered only because $115.8M of non-bond money, bond premiums, a partner contribution, interest earnings, absorbed the overrun [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-1-phase-2 p14]. Grant High School went from an $88M budget to roughly $159M, up around 80 percent [bond-audit-bond-construction-program-audit-4-of-4 p33-37; bond-audit-year-1-phase-2 p14]. Franklin finished 38 percent over its original budget, Roosevelt 49 percent [bond-audit-bond-construction-program-audit-4-of-4 p37].
- **2017 program ($790M authorized):** the program was built by cutting roughly $100M from the district's own internal cost model before the ballot, an unexplained gap the auditors flagged in their first report. It then grew to about $1.1 billion [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-3 narrative]. **Benson Polytechnic: $202M at the ballot, $421.2M estimated at completion, up 108 percent** [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6 p13-14], with the auditors decomposing the Year 4 jump into escalation ($21M), scope ($17M), and a $14M "budgeting error" [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-4 p14], and warning in 2025 that pending claims could push it higher [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6 p14]. The gap was plugged by redirecting 2020 bond money. Against that: Lincoln finished $16.9M *under* its revised budget, and Kellogg and McDaniel landed close to revised targets [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6 p13]. The machine can hit budgets; it did not hit Benson's.
- **2020 program ($1,208M authorized):** Jefferson went from $311M at the ballot to a $491M schematic-design estimate by September 2024, at which point design was paused and opening slipped two years [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6 p21-22]. Cleveland and Ida B. Wells, sold to voters as planning allocations, produced full-build estimates near $481M and $455M before being re-targeted at $360M each, with $340M apiece assumed from the 2025 bond [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6 p27-28]. The **Center for Black Student Excellence: $60M allocated in 2020, zero dollars spent through February 2025**, its director hired only in fall 2024, flagged by the auditors three years running [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6 p21, p33-34]. The technology program slipped four years, to 2029 [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-6 p32-33].

**The strongest defense:** 2020-2023 construction inflation was violent and national; seismic and code scope grew for real reasons; litigation against Benson's contractor may recover money; every district building in that window got hurt; and not building has costs too.

**The verdict standard:** decompose each overrun into market escalation, which is a forecasting problem, and the residual, scope drift, budgeting errors, schedule slippage, which is controllable. Only the residual is eligible for **Shown**. On that standard: the Benson "budgeting error" named by the district's own auditors, the pre-ballot $100M cut from the internal model in 2017 [bond-audit-bond-performance-audit-year-1-phase-1 p13-14], the pattern, three bonds running, of ballot numbers that the district's own subsequent estimates blow through within two years, and a $60M racial-justice commitment that sat unspent for four years, are **Shown**, on the locked ledger. The honest sentence about scale: the controllable residual is meaningfully smaller than the headline overruns, and it is still very large money. What it cost was never teachers. It was bond capacity: fewer schools fixed per bond, and a bigger ask each cycle, which is exactly what the 2025 measure's $1.83 billion is.

Ballot-number lowballing has a compounding cost that outlasts any project: it spends trust. Three cycles of it is a pattern voters will eventually price in.

## W2 · The carrying cost of the footprint (movable ledger, structural)

Priced in section 09. The verdict is not "small schools are waste"; they are a purchase communities may choose. The verdict is **A cost of delay**: the enrollment forecasts were public and unambiguous by 2021-22, the consolidation process began in fall 2026, and every intervening year the General Fund paid full fixed costs on thousands of empty seats while cutting classroom positions. The precise annual figure requires the district's own closure-savings model, which is not public (request R5); on any plausible per-building fixed cost, four to five years of delay across dozens of under-enrolled buildings is a General Fund sum in the tens of millions, borne concurrently with the cuts in section 07. We decline to print a fake-precise number; we name the years.

## W3 · Contested allocations: central office, contracts, consultants (movable ledger)

The evidence, laid out in section 08: instruction cut more than twice as fast in percentage terms as central business support across FY22-FY26; a General Fund purchased-services line steady near 12 to 13 percent; an all-funds contracting surge that is two-thirds bond; specific contracts the CBRC has now flagged in three separate years, including MAP assessment subscriptions averaging $218,000 a year that largely duplicate a free state service (the state covers grades 3-8; the committee recommended full discontinuation in 2025 and a 3-8 replacement in 2026) [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p10; cbrc-2025-26-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p6], and management-services growth of $74.4M (199 percent) in one proposed year [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p9]; a new senior administrative position at $224,000 to $239,000 created in the same budget that cut 247 staff [cbrc-2025-26-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p6]; and a district claim of 65 central office positions cut in a year when the whole support-services function, which mixes central and school-based supports, fell 0.5 percent, with the central-only line "not clearly presented" in the committee's words [same, p6].

**The strongest defense:** central functions absorb state and federal mandates that grew all decade; some spending coded "support services" is counselors, coaches, and campus safety, not suits; contracting can genuinely beat hiring once you count the 23 percent pension tail on every FTE; and the function-code recodings across years make raw comparisons treacherous.

**The verdict standard** we adopt, and commend to the district's critics: peer-relative and like-for-like, on Oregon's own chart of accounts, against Beaverton, Salem-Keizer, and the statewide average. Where PPS runs above the peer median on central-support share for consecutive years *without a published justification*, the verdict is **unexplained overhead**: not proven waste, but the burden of proof shifts to the district. We cannot complete that test today, because the comparable state data lags two years and the district publishes no benchmarking of its own. That absence is not our failure to find an answer. It is the answer. The 2019 state audit's exact words about PPS budget documents: "limited program detail, few performance measures, no benchmarking against other districts, little detail on changes in staffing and spending over time" [sos-audit-2019-01]. Seven years on, a reader still cannot compute central-office share from PPS's own publications, and the CBRC has asked for that disclosure three years running.

Individual items: the MAP contract is as close to textbook as this record gets, a paid service largely duplicating a free state-aligned one for grades 3-8, flagged by the citizen committee in 2023, 2025, and 2026, still in the budget: **Shown**, small dollars, telling. The Procedeo management contract ($61.5M over five years, per reporting) and the 199 percent management-services jump: **Likely, pending one document**, the contracts themselves (request R10).

## W4 · Process waste: money lost to bad information and late decisions

This is where the document lands hardest, because it is fully documented, fully inside PPS control, and it converts every other problem into a more expensive one.

The record, as corrected: the district's quarterly and monthly reports [financeInterim; pps-q1-fy2025-26 through pps-p11-fy2025-26] show its own FY2025-26 year-end forecast swinging from $41.3M to $17.7M to $26.8M to $34.6M inside one year, with the December report attributing part of the drop to staffing additions and unrealized vacancy savings. The mid-year deficit was therefore visible in the district's own publications while it happened, confirmed by the citizen committee [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p5] and reported in the press at roughly $10 million with a furloughs-or-layoffs offer to unions [news-opb-2026-02-24-midyear; news-ww-2026-02-27-furloughs, press], in a year whose budget TSCC had certified without objection. A FY2026-27 gap the district's own review cycle stated as both $50 million and $56.3 million [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p6, p9]. (Our earlier claim that no interim statements existed was wrong and is retracted; the census missed the finance page that carries them.) A citizen committee given nine working days, four years running, that this year questioned whether the budget's central assumptions, a 1 percent cost-of-living adjustment "significantly lower than any COLA the district has negotiated with its labor partners in recent memory," and optimistic property-tax revenue, were realistic at adoption [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p4]. A pension stabilization reserve spent in a single year against a multi-year rate cliff the actuaries had scheduled in advance [tscc-review-fy2026 p10]. A budget calendar amended mid-cycle [budget-fy2026-27-calendar-amended]. Nineteen internal contradictions across the oversight bodies' own published tables, an audited report whose statistical section carries an impossible enrollment number, and a budget book whose appropriations attachment is unreadable in its own text layer.

And over it all, the audit. In January 2019 the Secretary of State made 26 recommendations to the state education department and PPS together, 15 of them to PPS, all accepted [sos-audit-2019-01]. The state's own 2022 follow-up scored 13 of the 15 as only "partially implemented" [sos-secretary-of-state-sos-recommendation-follow-up-report], and the specific artifacts the audit asked for on financial legibility, a readable budget summary and multi-year trend data, still do not exist in the FY2026-27 book, which is why this document had to build the section 07 table from scratch. There has been no state performance audit since. The district's internal performance audit office has published, by the titles in its own public index, about a dozen reports since 2019, none of them titled on budgeting, forecasting, or position control (the reports themselves are registered but not yet read; we characterize only their titles).

**The strongest defense:** biennial state revenue genuinely is hard to forecast; ESSER rules whipsawed every district; the budget office itself has been cut; mid-year correction is at least responsiveness.

**The verdict:** **Shown**, recast by the correction. The waste is not darkness; it is forecast volatility, weak position control, and the absence of any mechanism that forces a public reconciliation when the forecast moves eight figures in a quarter. Mid-year cuts buy less per dollar than June cuts; emergency patches land on whatever can be moved fast; reserves spent against a known cliff are borrowed from next year at full price; and assumptions the citizen committee flagged as optimistic at adoption became the whipsaw the quarterly reports then recorded. The CBRC's warning stands as the summary: a second consecutive mid-year deficit "would cause significant long-term damage to the public's trust in PPS" [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p5].

If this document changes one thing, it should be this: **make the books decision-grade.** A forecast-accuracy scorecard, a revision log, seasonal comparators, position control that reconciles monthly, and a standing rule that a material forecast change gets a public explanation and a named response, none of which requires a dollar of new revenue.

## W5 · One-time money spent on ongoing things: the ESSER braid

The facts are in section 07: roughly $107.5M in relief, deliberately braided into continuing programs, $36.3M still unspent seven months before the deadline, and a cut sequence afterward that tracks the withdrawal almost dollar for dollar.

Spending one-time money on children in crisis was not waste; it was arguably the point of the money, and federal guidance encouraged it. The adjudicable question is **disclosure at decision time**: did the district show the public the cliff on the same page as the programs? The contemporaneous record answers. The district's own citizen committee wrote in spring 2023 that the funding sources were "hiding the looming shortfall ... from the general public" [cbrc-2023-24-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p3], and repeated the structural warning in 2025 in one sentence: "The district cannot afford to make temporary programs permanent without permanent funding" [cbrc-2025-26-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p10]. The choice to ride one-time money was defensible. The failure to print the cliff beside the promise was not. Verdict: the spending, **A choice, not waste**; the presentation, filed under W4, where it belongs.

## What this document declines to call waste

1. **Bond dollars "instead of teachers."** Legally impossible, every time, no exceptions.
2. **Teacher compensation, including the strike settlement.** A bargained market price for the people who are the service. Its affordability is a solvency question, section 11 adjudicates the causal fight, but dollars reaching classrooms through teachers is the opposite of waste.
3. **Special education spending above the state's 11 percent cap.** PPS serves the students in front of it, at 17 percent; the shortfall is a state formula failure landing on the General Fund. Calling it waste blames the district for educating disabled children.
4. **Small schools as such.** A purchase. See W2 for the part that does earn a verdict.
5. **Reserves at the 5 percent floor.** Holding a minimum reserve is not hoarding; the fights over tapping it are values fights.
6. **High per-pupil spending versus other states.** An input level, usually computed on incomparable accounting bases.
7. **Compression losses.** The tax constitution doing what voters wrote in 1990 and 1997. A leak to fix in Salem, not district behavior.

# 11 · Seven arguments, adjudicated

Each argument below gets both sides at full strength, then where this analysis lands. Two rules govern: every adjudication names the test that would settle it and the documents that run the test, and arithmetic outranks balance. Where the numbers settle a question, we say so; the two-sided format is reserved for genuine value conflicts.

**D1 · "PPS is broke" versus "PPS is hiding money."** The teachers union's analysis [pat-funding-memo, advocacy] holds that the district under-forecasts revenue, added 149 central office positions from 2019 to 2024, and could spend tens of millions of fund balance within its own reserve policy. The district holds that the structural deficit is real and driven by enrollment, PERS, and expiring one-time funds. Split the claim into its three checkable parts. Revenue under-forecasting: testable against our budget-versus-actual series; the CBRC this year worried assumptions were too *optimistic*, the opposite direction; unresolved pending the full actuals comparison. The 149 positions: **Likely, pending one document**, position-control records (request R4); directionally consistent with the instructional-coach and administrator growth the CBRC documented in 2023-24. Reserve headroom: arithmetic now answers it; reserves sit at the 5 percent policy floor, roughly $41 million against an $862 million fund [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p36], and the pension reserve is already spent [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p138-140; tscc-review-fy2026 p10]. There is no hidden pot. **Landing: the structural deficit is real; the union is right that central growth during the fat years deserved more scrutiny than it got; both truths fit in one sentence.**

**D2 · The $2.77 billion framing.** Critics: the budget has never been bigger while classrooms bleed. District: most of that is bond capital. The numbers settle this one, section 03's table is the whole adjudication, and the finding worth keeping is about communication: **the district owns the confusion.** The one-page decomposition that would kill this argument forever, operating versus capital versus debt, side by side in plain language, is precisely what the 2019 audit requested and what the CBRC asked for again in 2025 and 2026. It still does not exist in any PPS publication. When an institution leaves a vacuum that simple, it cannot complain about what fills it.

**D3 · The levy: 660 promised, 744 delivered, and falling.** Is the levy overdelivering (744 positions against a 660 promise) or eroding (916 down to a projected 718)? Both, because the baselines differ: the 2024 campaign promised conservatively against compression-reduced receipts; the erosion series measures against 2019's actual delivery. The honest statement for the 2029 renewal campaign, and the trust ledger it will run on: the levy does exactly what it legally must, funds only teachers, is audited clean every year, and buys about 22 percent fewer of them than it did six years ago because compression eats a growing share and teachers cost 43 percent more. Also on the ledger: two consecutive official reviews reported 851 and then 922 positions for the same year, and this year's budget shows about $104 million of levy income with no function-and-object breakdown, which the CBRC says makes its own compliance check "difficult to undertake" [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p4]. A levy whose entire premise is trust should be the best-documented money in the district. It is not.

**D4 · "PPS hides state money."** A recurring public claim, fact-checked by local press and checkable in the books: state formula revenue appears as intergovernmental revenue in the General Fund, on the page. **The claim is false.** The kernel worth conceding is D2: opacity this durable manufactures its own conspiracy theories, and every year without a readable budget summary regenerates them.

**D5 · The equity staffing formula and the federal lawsuit.** Since at least FY2016-17 [cbrc-2023-24-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p8] PPS has set aside a share of school staffing, once 8 percent, halved to 4 percent for K-8 in 2024-25 [cbrc-2025-26-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p2], for schools serving concentrated poverty and historically underserved students, with race among the qualifying categories. In October 2025 the Center for Individual Rights sued in federal court, alleging racial discrimination (press-reported; docket check pending, section 13). This document takes no position on live litigation, and the reader should distrust anyone who prejudges a federal case in a policy paper. Two things are fairly said now. The dollars at stake are small against the rhetoric: a few percent of the staffing allocation. And the empirical question that is fair game regardless of the case's outcome, whether a decade of equity allocation measurably closed the gaps it targeted, has never been answered by the district, though its own citizen committee asked for exactly that return-on-investment analysis in 2023 and 2024 and the allocation was halved in 2024 without one. A district that will not measure its values spending leaves that spending undefended in every courtroom, including the court of public opinion.

**D6 · "Closures will fix the deficit."** They will not. Per-building operating savings are real but small against a structural gap of more than $65 million [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p11]; the district's own model is unpublished (request R5); and closures spend enormous political capital, can accelerate enrollment loss when handled badly, and land unevenly across the city. The opposite claim, that closures are needless cruelty, prices empty seats at zero, which five years of concurrent classroom cuts disprove. **Landing: consolidation is portfolio hygiene, a necessary correction of geometry to enrollment, and anyone selling it as either deficit salvation or pure vandalism is selling something.** The test that matters is whether savings are redirected measurably into remaining classrooms, which requires the model to be public first.

**D7 · "The strike broke the budget."** The most charged causal claim in the city, so it gets the most mechanical treatment. What the record supports: the press-reported settlement cost, roughly $175 million over three years, implies on the order of $58 million a year against a General Fund then around $830 million [news-opb-2023-11-29-strike-faq, press; derived: gf_series], immediately preceding the ESSER expiration and the PERS cliff; the district said at settlement it would require deep cuts, and the cut sequence followed. What the record equally supports: the structural deficit predates the strike, the CBRC was warning about one-time money hiding the shortfall six months *before* the strike, enrollment revenue loss and pension escalation are each in the same order of magnitude, and the district did not provide its own citizen committee the cost information that would have let anyone evaluate affordability during the negotiation [cbrc-2024-25-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p11]. **Landing: the strike settlement is one of three roughly comparable pressures, alongside enrollment and PERS, stacked on a base that one-time money had been concealing. "The strike did it" and "the strike is blameless" are both propaganda. The full decomposition awaits the cost model the district has never published, request R3.**

# 12 · The decade ahead: what to watch

Six things will decide whether the next ten years repeat the last ten. Each comes with its tripwire, the one number that, when published, tells you which way it broke.

1. **The FY2027-28 budget, spring 2027.** The district projects another gap of more than $65 million [budget-fy2026-27-vol1 p11]. Tripwire: whether the adopted General Fund falls a second consecutive year, and whether the district can state one gap number per cycle, unlike this year's $50 million and $56.3 million appearing in the same review [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review p6, p9].
2. **The closure decision, due end of 2026.** Up to 20 schools per press reporting [news-ww-2026-08-26-20-schools, press], effective 2027-28. Tripwires: publication of the savings model before the vote, and a board resolution tying named savings to named classroom reinvestment. Absent both, expect maximum pain for minimum arithmetic.
3. **The mid-year test, February 2027.** The CBRC's line about a second consecutive mid-year deficit doing "significant long-term damage to the public's trust" is now the standing benchmark. Tripwire: any mid-year supplemental budget or "discovered" shortfall, which would confirm the information blackout is a policy, not an accident.
4. **The 2025 bond's first real audit cycle.** Jefferson redesigned after a $491 million estimate against a $311 million promise; Cleveland and Ida B. Wells re-targeted at $360 million each. Tripwire: the next Sjoberg Evashenk report's estimate-at-completion for Jefferson against the reduced target, and whether the Center for Black Student Excellence finally reports spending against its $60 million.
5. **PERS, 2027-29 rates.** The side accounts that held PPS's rate near 4 percent are expiring into a rate near 23 percent, and the stabilization reserve is gone. Tripwire: the PERS board's 2027-29 school-pool rate adoption, and whether Salem separates pension costs from the school fund as the CBRC urged.
6. **The 2029 levy renewal.** The trust ledger from D3 comes due: compression projected past $53 million, positions drifting toward 700, average teacher cost past $152,000. Tripwire: whether the campaign states the effective rate, near $1.51 and falling, next to the $1.99 headline, and whether Salem has moved on compression by then.

The state layer moves slower but moves. The Quality Education Model commission was reported in August 2026 to say current-service-level schooling statewide needs about $2.5 billion more per biennium [news-opb-2026-08-12-qem, press]. Whether Oregon has ever funded to its own model is itself contested: the legislature's own adequacy report claims the 2025-27 appropriation "effectively closes the funding gap" [jpea-adequacy-report-2025], a claim the commission's reported 2026 numbers dispute. The Education Stability Fund that PPS asked the state to tap in spring 2026, an ask its citizen committee endorsed [cbrc-2026-27-cbrc-proposed-budget-review], was not granted; expect the ask again.

# 13 · What we cannot know

What a public body declines to publish is a decision about the public. This section is the census of those decisions, organized by theme, each with why it matters and the specific ask that would fix it. The records requests are drafted and ready in the corpus; none has been sent.

**A. The year in progress is visible but unsteered** *(corrected August 30, 2026)*.
- Interim reports exist, quarterly to FY2023-24 and monthly for FY2025-26, correcting this document's earlier claim that none did (our census inspected the annual financial-reports page and missed the finance page that carries them). What still does not exist: a forecast-accuracy standard, a revision log, or any mechanism forcing a public reconciliation when the forecast swings, as it did by $24 million in one quarter of FY2025-26. **R1 as originally drafted is withdrawn; the reframed ask is the forecast-accuracy and reconciliation standard of Decision 1.**

**B. The debt is invisible through PPS channels.**
- Not one bond official statement, rating report, or continuing-disclosure filing appears on any PPS website; they exist only on the municipal bond market's EMMA system, where no parent will ever look. Why it matters: the district's own risk disclosures to investors are more candid than anything it publishes to voters. **Ask: post them; meanwhile we pull them from EMMA.**

**C. The watchdogs are quiet or unarmed.**
- No Secretary of State performance audit since January 2019, across a strike, an ESSER cycle, and five years of cuts.
- The internal Office of Internal Performance Audit, created in 2019, has published roughly one report a year, none on budgeting, forecasting, position control, or central-office cost.
- The CBRC gets nine working days (documented in its own reports three years running, after a related complaint the year before).
- TSCC's FY2026-27 review of PPS had not been posted at this writing. **Ask R8.**

**D. The record is decaying.**
- The district's audit index mislabels its bond audit series by year: the FY2021-22 audit exists (it is the "Year 4" report, issued February 2023, and it is in our corpus), but no audit report was *issued* in calendar 2022 at all, a publication-cadence gap of nearly sixteen months during active construction. (An earlier draft of this project asked for a "missing" 2022 audit; the document was in hand all along under another name. Request R2 is withdrawn.)
- No cost model for the roughly $175 million strike settlement has ever been published; the figure exists only in press statements [news-opb-2023-11-29-strike-faq, press], though the signed contract is public [cba-pat-2023-2026]. **Ask R3.**
- The 2011-13 teacher contract is missing from the district's own contract archive.
- There is no public index of budget amendments or supplemental budgets; reconstructing mid-year changes requires reading every board packet. **Ask R9.**
- Documents live behind opaque links that have broken once already; the state's original URL for its 2019 PPS audit (sos.oregon.gov/audits/Documents/2019-01.pdf) returned a 404 on our August 29, 2026 fetch, and the report survives publicly on the district's own mirror.

**E. Specific numbers still unverified, carried honestly.**
- Position-control records that would settle the central-office growth claims (R4).
- The closure savings model (R5).
- The Procedeo contract and deliverables (R10).
- The 88 percent administration-growth figure for 2000-2019, press-reported, pending primary verification.
- ODE's computed local-option exclusion limit for PPS, which determines whether the 2024 levy renewal is fully additive at the margin (R7).
- The FY2022-23 CBRC annual review, which exists only as a scanned image no machine can read, and the blank narrative pages of the 2021-22 levy review. OCR pending.
- The internal performance audit office's reports (registered, unread) and the pre-2016 budget books and ACFRs (posted by the district, outside this document's horizon, registered and archived but not fetched).

A note on what this list is not. It is not an accusation of concealment. Most of these gaps are the ordinary entropy of an institution that has been cutting its own administrative capacity for years. But the effect does not care about the intent: the public cannot answer the basic questions of section 01 from the district's own publications, and this document exists because of that.

# 14 · The doctrine

The venue-portfolio dive ends with a doctrine; this one earns the same. One sentence, then the commitments that operationalize it, then the question.

**The doctrine of the movable dollar:**

> Count every dollar in the open, say plainly which ones can move, and make every dollar that can move prove, in public, every spring, that nothing else it could buy would do more for a student who is here now.

**Ten commitments** that follow from it, any of which a board member could move as a resolution:

1. Make the monthly and quarterly statements decision-grade: forecast-accuracy scoring, revision logs, and public reconciliation of material changes. No new money required, and it retires the whipsaw era.
2. Publish the one-page budget: operating, capital, debt, side by side, with the ledger tags. Kill D2 forever.
3. Publish the trend table, section 07's spine, in every budget book. The state asked in 2019.
4. Sort every public dollar figure by ledger before arguing about it, and never claim a locked dollar as a movable one, for any audience, in any direction.
5. Give the citizen reviewers the weeks the work needs, and answer their recommendations in writing, item by item, before adoption. Nine days, three years running, is a choice.
6. Benchmark central costs against Beaverton, Salem-Keizer, and the state annually, or accept that others will do it less charitably.
7. When spending one-time money on ongoing things, print the cliff on the same page as the promise.
8. Publish the closure savings model before asking any community to accept a closure.
9. Treat an unremediated audit finding as a standing debt with a date on it, reported annually until paid.
10. Put the levy's effective rate next to its nominal rate everywhere the levy is mentioned. Trust is the levy's entire technology.

**The annual question:** *Does the next dollar reach a student, and can you show me?*

# 15 · Method, independence, corrections

**What is ours and what is sourced.** Every load-bearing number traces to a named document and page in the project corpus; the registry lists 199 sources, of which 115 are fetched and checksummed. Judgments, the ledger tags, the waste verdicts, the adjudications, the doctrine, are Portland Civic Lab analysis and labeled as such. Figures we could not trace to a primary document are carried with a "reported, pending verification" label or excluded.

**The Maine warning.** portlandschools.org and portlandk12.org belong to Portland, Maine, whose school budget passes by voter referendum and pollutes search results for this topic. Oregon school budgets never go to a referendum. Every figure here is domain-verified to Oregon primary sources.

**Secondary figures pending primary verification, by name:** the 88 percent administration-growth claim (2000-2019, press); the roughly $175 million strike settlement cost (district statement via press [news-opb-2023-11-29-strike-faq]); the 149 added central positions (union analysis [pat-funding-memo]); the $61.5 million Procedeo contract value (press [news-opb-2026-05-13-contracts]). Each is labeled where used.

**Advocacy sources are labeled as advocacy** wherever used: the teachers union's funding memo, the Center for Individual Rights case page, Cascade Policy Institute publications, campaign websites. They supply claims and framings, never uncontested facts.

**Known defects in the source record**, disclosed rather than smoothed: nineteen logged cross-document contradictions in TSCC tables; an ACFR whose statistical section, which sits outside the independent auditor's opinion, prints an impossible 52,380 enrollment for 2022-23 (we dropped it by continuity rule, used the TSCC value in section 07's table, and flag it here for the district; the audit firm, Talbot, Korvola & Warwick, has held the engagement for at least a decade, itself worth a governance conversation about auditor rotation); a budget book whose appropriations attachment is unreadable in its own text layer; image-only tables in several oversight documents whose numbers are effectively unpublished.

**Definitions discipline.** Enrollment means unduplicated October headcount unless stated; ADMw is the state's weighted count; the two differ by thousands. Adopted budgets are plans; ACFR figures are audited actuals; every table says which basis it uses. Real-dollar figures use the national CPI-U deflated to calendar-2026 terms, with each fiscal year mapped to its starting calendar year; the 2026 deflator is provisional. Oregon chart-of-accounts figures and federal survey figures are never mixed in one comparison.

**Live litigation.** Allegations in CIR v. PPS are attributed as allegations; this document takes no position on the merits and stamps its docket knowledge as of August 2026.

**Independence.** Portland Civic Lab is unaffiliated with and unfunded by PPS, any employee union, any bond contractor or vendor named here, and any campaign. See portlandciviclab.org/independence for the Lab's standing disclosures.

**Corrections.** We invite them, specifically and publicly: PPS finance staff, PAT researchers, CBRC members, TSCC staff, and the district's auditors are all better positioned than we are on individual figures, and this document's data layer makes every number checkable to a page. Corrections will be logged visibly in the published version. The whole premise of this work is that the numbers should be checkable by strangers; that includes ours.
